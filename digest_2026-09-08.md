# Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models: The Forward Process, Score Matching, and Learning to Reverse Gaussian Corruption

**Date:** 2026-09-08
**Arc:** 1/2

The curriculum has moved steadily through discriminative vision systems — from contrastive and masked pretraining objectives that produce strong feature representations, to foundation models like CLIP and SAM that generalize broadly across downstream tasks without task-specific supervision. All of those systems share a common orientation: they learn to encode, classify, detect, or segment, but they do not learn to synthesize new images from scratch. To understand the modern landscape of vision research fully, one must also understand the generative paradigm, and no framework has been as influential in the past several years as denoising diffusion probabilistic models. The core idea is elegant: rather than training a generator against a discriminator in an adversarial minimax game, or maximizing a variational lower bound through a bottleneck latent code, diffusion models learn by gradually destroying information in a structured way and then training a neural network to reverse that destruction step by step.

The forward process in a diffusion model is a fixed Markov chain that progressively adds Gaussian noise to a clean data point over T discrete timesteps. At each step t, a small amount of noise is added according to a predetermined noise schedule — a sequence of positive scalars β₁, β₂, …, β_T, each much less than one, that collectively determine how quickly the data is corrupted. After enough steps the data distribution collapses to an isotropic Gaussian regardless of the original content: any image, no matter how structured, is eventually indistinguishable from pure noise. The choice of schedule is consequential; a linear schedule from β₁ = 0.0001 to β_T = 0.02 was used in the original DDPM work, though later research found cosine schedules that improve training stability at high resolutions. A crucial algebraic identity makes the entire procedure tractable: because any finite sum of independent Gaussians is itself Gaussian, the noisy image at an arbitrary timestep t can be expressed directly in terms of the original clean image x₀ and a single noise vector ε drawn from a standard normal. If one defines ᾱ_t as the product of (1 − β_s) for all s from 1 through t, the marginal distribution of x_t conditioned on x₀ is Gaussian with mean sqrt(ᾱ_t) x₀ and variance (1 − ᾱ_t). This closed-form expression eliminates the need to simulate the chain sequentially during training: at each gradient step one samples a random timestep, corrupts a clean training image to that noise level in a single operation, and asks the model to undo the corruption.

Ho, Jain, and Abbeel (2020) parameterize the learned reverse step as a Gaussian whose mean is predicted by a neural network conditioned on the noisy image and the timestep. The full variational lower bound on the log-likelihood is expressible as a sum of KL divergences between the tractable forward posterior and the learned reverse distributions at each step, but this objective is noisy and difficult to optimize directly. The key contribution of the paper is a simplified training target: rather than predicting the denoised image or the reverse mean directly, the network is trained to predict the noise vector ε that was added to produce x_t. The loss is simply the mean-squared error between the predicted noise and the actual noise that was injected. This reparameterization is more than a computational convenience; it has a precise statistical interpretation that connects DDPM to a completely independent line of research.

Song and Ermon (2019) had developed score-based generative modeling from first principles. The score of a distribution is the gradient of the log-probability density with respect to the data point — a vector field that points in the direction of increasing density at every location in data space. If one could estimate this vector field accurately, sampling could proceed by following it uphill through Langevin dynamics, a process that provably converges to samples from the target distribution. The difficulty is that computing the score requires knowing the partition function of the distribution, which is generally intractable. Score matching bypasses this by using the identity that the expected squared difference between the model score and the data score can be evaluated without computing the partition function, relying instead on samples from the data distribution. Song and Ermon extended this idea to a noise-conditional score network trained simultaneously across many noise levels, which is necessary because the score at high noise levels is well-defined and learnable while the score in low-density regions of the clean data distribution is poorly estimated. The connection to DDPM noise prediction is exact: the score of the noisy distribution q(x_t | x₀) with respect to x_t equals −ε / sqrt(1 − ᾱ_t), so predicting ε and predicting the score are equivalent up to a known scaling factor. Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon, and Poole (2021) later showed that both frameworks are special cases of a continuous-time stochastic differential equation, a unification that also enabled a companion ordinary differential equation whose deterministic trajectories connect clean data to Gaussian noise and back — the foundation of fast samplers.

The architecture of the noise predictor in the original DDPM is a U-Net, and this choice is not arbitrary. The encoder–decoder structure with skip connections matches the hierarchical nature of the denoising task: the network needs both global context to understand what coarse structure should be recovered and local precision to reconstruct fine detail, and skip connections make encoder features at each resolution available to the corresponding decoder level without having to pass them through a bottleneck. This is precisely the same motivation that made U-Net the default for medical image segmentation. Timestep conditioning is injected by mapping the scalar t through sinusoidal positional embeddings — borrowing directly from the transformer position encoding scheme covered earlier in the curriculum — projecting to the same channel dimension as each feature map, and adding element-wise before each residual block. Later versions of this architecture replace the purely convolutional blocks in the middle of the U-Net with self-attention layers, which allow the model to reason about global spatial relationships at the bottleneck resolution and were found to substantially improve sample quality on natural images.

The sampling procedure at inference time follows the learned reverse Markov chain starting from a sample of pure Gaussian noise x_T and iterating the reverse step for all T timesteps. Because each reverse step is conditioned only on the previous noisy sample and the timestep, the chain must be simulated sequentially; there is no way to parallelize across time. With T = 1000, this requires one thousand full forward passes through the noise predictor network, making inference several orders of magnitude slower than a single forward pass through a classification or segmentation model. This bottleneck directly motivated the development of denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIM), which observe that the score network implicitly defines a deterministic ODE over the noise schedule and that this ODE can be discretized much more coarsely. By taking larger steps along the deterministic trajectory, DDIM reduces the number of network evaluations to fifty or one hundred while achieving sample quality comparable to the full Markov chain, a ten-to-twenty-fold speedup that made diffusion models practical for interactive applications.

What the training procedure reveals about the structure of image distributions is as interesting as the practical results. The noise prediction loss at each timestep level contributes differently to the final perceptual quality of generated samples. At the highest noise levels, when x_t is nearly indistinguishable from pure noise, the model must recover global structure — the general layout, the positions of large objects, the overall color distribution. At the lowest noise levels, when x_t is already very close to a clean image, the model refines fine texture and sharp edges. This implicit separation of frequency content by timestep means the model organizes the learning problem by spatial scale without any explicit multi-scale supervision: low-frequency components are determined early in the reverse chain, and high-frequency components are added late. The next digest will examine what happens when the diffusion process is moved out of pixel space entirely and into a compressed latent space learned by a separate autoencoder, how classifier-free guidance enables text prompts and other conditioning signals to steer generation at inference time, and what this shift reveals about the scalability and compositionality of the diffusion framework.
