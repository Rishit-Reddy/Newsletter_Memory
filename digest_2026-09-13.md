# Video Vision Transformers: Tube Tokenization, Temporal Attention, and the Factorization Question

**Date:** 2026-09-13
**Arc:** 1/2 — Video Vision Transformers

---

The last two digests traced the evolution of 3D scene representation from implicit coordinate networks (NeRF) to explicit Gaussian primitives (3D Gaussian Splatting). Both frameworks reconstruct static moments in three-dimensional space from collections of 2D observations, and in doing so they treat time as an indexing variable rather than a genuine dimension of the signal. Real visual data does not cooperate with this simplification. In video, the photometric identity of a pixel cannot be disentangled from the motion of the camera and the motion of the scene; a cardiac scan is not a repeated photograph of the same organ, and a surgical video is not a slideshow of instruments frozen mid-gesture. Extending the Vision Transformer, which was designed for single-frame spatial reasoning, to the temporal domain forces a reckoning with a question that image models never had to face: how should attention range over both space and time simultaneously, and at what computational cost?

The most direct extension of the standard ViT to video is conceptually simple. If patches are the atoms of spatial tokenization, then spacetime tubes are the natural atoms of temporal tokenization. A tube is a spatially contiguous patch extracted consistently across multiple consecutive frames: a 16×16-pixel region at a fixed spatial location sampled over, say, 2 or 4 consecutive frames. Just as a 2D patch becomes a flat vector by concatenating pixel values and then projecting with a learned linear map, a 3D tube becomes a token by concatenating voxel values across its temporal extent and projecting them similarly. This produces a sequence of tokens whose length scales with the product of the number of spatial patches and the number of temporal positions sampled, and that sequence is then passed directly into a standard Transformer. The approach is elegant in its minimalism, but the length of the resulting token sequence — growing as T × H/p × W/p where T is the number of frames and p is the patch size — pushes the self-attention computation into territory that becomes prohibitive at any interesting temporal extent. A single 8-frame 224×224 video clip tokenized with a 16-pixel patch yields 8 × 14 × 14 = 1568 tokens; a 32-frame clip yields 6272. Full self-attention over these sequences is feasible in research but expensive in practice.

The central design challenge of video transformers is therefore managing the interaction between temporal extent and computational cost without sacrificing the representational richness that makes full self-attention so powerful in the image domain. Several strategies have been proposed, and they differ in exactly how they factor or restrict the attention computation. The most influential early answer came from the TimeSformer architecture (Bertasius, Wang, and Torresani, 2021), which decomposed full spacetime attention into two independent factorized operations applied sequentially within each Transformer block. In the spatial attention step, each token attends only to all tokens at the same temporal position — effectively running standard 2D ViT attention independently for each frame. In the temporal attention step, each token attends only to tokens at the same spatial position across all frames — running a 1D sequence model along the time axis for each spatial location independently. The total attention complexity for this scheme is O(T · H' · W' · (H'·W') + T · H' · W' · T) where H' and W' are the spatial grid dimensions after patch extraction, as opposed to O((T · H' · W')²) for full spacetime attention. The two terms correspond to spatial attention within each frame and temporal attention across frames at each spatial location, respectively. The key observation motivating this decomposition is that spatial and temporal context can often be gathered separately without much loss: identifying what an object is requires attending to its spatial neighborhood in a given frame, while tracking how it moves requires attending to the same spatial region across time. The decomposition is an inductive bias, not a proven fact, but it proves empirically competitive with full attention at a fraction of the cost.

The Video Swin Transformer (Liu et al., 2022) takes a different approach rooted in its 2D predecessor's core contribution: hierarchical processing with shifted window attention. In the image Swin Transformer, attention is restricted to non-overlapping local windows, and windows shift between layers to allow information to propagate across window boundaries. The video variant extends this to three-dimensional windows operating over patches in space and time jointly. A spatiotemporal window of size T_win × H_win × W_win contains all the tokens that fall within a local volumetric region of the video, and self-attention runs only within each window. The shifted-window mechanism then displaces window boundaries between consecutive blocks by half their size in each spatial dimension and a corresponding amount along the time axis, enabling cross-window communication without expanding the per-block attention cost. Because temporal locality aligns naturally with the short-range dependencies of motion — nearby frames share more visual context than distant ones — the video Swin's local temporal attention is not as punishing a constraint as one might expect. The hierarchical pooling that Swin applies spatially in the image domain is extended to spatiotemporally in the video domain, gradually reducing the token count as layers deepen and allowing higher-level features to integrate information over longer ranges.

A third design point, explored systematically in the ViViT framework (Arnab et al., 2021), enumerated the space of architectural variants more explicitly. ViViT proposed four candidate architectures ranging from a pure spatial-then-temporal stack (process each frame independently with a ViT, then run a temporal Transformer over the resulting frame-level representations) to a unified model with full joint spacetime attention. The intermediate factorized encoder model, which interleaves spatial and temporal attention heads in the same blocks, proved most competitive with full attention while remaining computationally tractable. The pure frame-stack variant was cheapest but sacrificed fine-grained temporal interaction; the full joint attention variant was most expressive but demanded pretraining at a scale that precluded routine use. This systematic comparison made ViViT useful not just as an architecture but as an empirical map of the tradeoff space.

What emerges from comparing these three frameworks is that the choice of factorization carries different implicit assumptions about the structure of motion in natural video. TimeSformer's separable attention assumes that spatial and temporal dependencies are largely independent — that knowing where to look in space at a given moment does not much depend on the temporal context, and vice versa. Video Swin assumes that the most important dependencies are local in spacetime, and that long-range information can be aggregated gradually as features are pooled across the hierarchy. ViViT's frame-then-temporal stack assumes that frame-level semantics can be abstracted before temporal reasoning begins, which is reasonable for action recognition (identifying that a frame contains a hand holding a racket) but potentially lossy for problems requiring fine-grained temporal reasoning (understanding how the hand grips the racket across a stroke). These are not merely computational trade-offs; they are prior beliefs about the geometry of visual information in time.

One aspect that all three approaches share is a heavy reliance on transfer from image-pretrained weights. Because video datasets are expensive to annotate and far smaller than the image datasets on which large ViTs are trained, the practical recipe for all three architectures involves initializing with weights from an ImageNet-pretrained ViT and then fine-tuning on video. Spatial attention weights transfer directly; temporal attention weights are typically initialized in a way that preserves the behavior of the pretrained spatial model at initialization — for example, by initializing temporal attention matrices such that each frame token attends exclusively to itself, reproducing the frame-independent ViT behavior before training begins. This warm-start strategy means that video ViTs inherit the powerful image representations learned at scale, with temporal reasoning gradually incorporated during fine-tuning. The dependence on image pretraining is significant because it bounds the architectural innovation available in the temporal dimension: whatever temporal attention mechanism is chosen, it must be compatible with a warm-start from purely spatial weights. This constraint also motivates the next natural question, which the arc will address tomorrow: whether self-supervised learning on video itself — without labels, and without relying on ImageNet pretraining as a foundation — can close the gap and produce representations that genuinely understand temporal structure rather than merely tolerating it.
