# Swin Transformer: Window-Based Self-Attention, Shifted Windows, and the Hierarchical Architecture That Unified Backbone Design

**Date:** 2026-09-23

---

Over the past several weeks we have traced a wide arc through vision architectures: the patch-tokenization mechanics of ViT, the encoder–decoder symmetry of U-Net, the two-stage and one-stage detection paradigms, and most recently the dilated convolutions and atrous spatial pyramid pooling at the heart of DeepLab's approach to semantic segmentation. Throughout that journey the name Swin Transformer has appeared as a footnote — mentioned as the backbone inside Swin-UNet, noted as the encoder behind Mask2Former, credited in passing when detection benchmarks cited hierarchical feature pyramids. Today we give it the treatment it deserves, because understanding Swin deeply reframes how you think about nearly every modern dense-prediction system.

The starting point is a concrete critique of the original Vision Transformer. ViT processes an image as a flat sequence of non-overlapping 16×16 patches, runs global multi-head self-attention across all of them, and produces a single feature map at stride 16 relative to the input. For a 224×224 image that means 196 tokens — perfectly tractable — but the quadratic cost of global attention means that moving to 512×512 input, as dense prediction requires, produces 1024 tokens and roughly a 27× increase in attention computation. More problematically, ViT's output is spatially uniform: every token lives at the same 1/16 scale, with no spatial hierarchy analogous to the four- or five-scale feature pyramid that ResNets produce naturally through stride-2 layers. Detection and segmentation heads — Mask R-CNN's RoI heads, FPN's top-down pathway, the deformable attention in Deformable DETR — are designed explicitly around multi-scale features, and ViT does not natively provide them. These two problems, quadratic complexity and the absence of a feature hierarchy, are what the Swin Transformer was designed to fix simultaneously.

Swin addresses the hierarchy problem first and most visibly. Rather than beginning with 16×16 patches, it partitions the image into very small 4×4 pixel patches and treats the concatenated pixel values of each tiny patch as an initial token embedding. A 224×224 image thus begins with 56×56 = 3136 tokens, far too many for global attention, but the hierarchy is immediately imposed through a patch merging operation applied at each of four stage transitions. At each transition, four spatially adjacent 2×2 tokens are concatenated into a single vector and linearly projected to double the channel dimension, halving spatial resolution. The result is a feature pyramid at strides 4, 8, 16, and 32 relative to the input, with progressively fewer tokens and deeper, more semantic channels at each level. This is the CNN feature pyramid recast in transformer language: early stages are spatially dense and shallow, later stages are spatially coarse and semantically rich. Connecting these four outputs to an FPN neck gives you exactly the multi-scale feature representation any detection or segmentation head expects, which is why Swin slots into Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN, and Mask2Former as a near-drop-in backbone replacement.

The complexity problem is solved through windowed self-attention. Within each stage, the spatial tokens are partitioned into non-overlapping M×M windows — typically M=7, giving 49 tokens per window — and multi-head self-attention is computed entirely within each window, independently. Because M is a fixed hyperparameter, the computation per window is O(M⁴) and the total cost over N tokens is O(NM²), growing linearly in N rather than quadratically. This is the crucial change for high-resolution feature maps: at the stride-4 stage of Swin, where token count is largest, windowed attention costs far less than the global alternative, while at the stride-32 stage the sequence is already short enough that the distinction barely matters. The cost of this locality is obvious: tokens in different windows cannot attend to each other, which would ordinarily prevent any global contextual reasoning.

The Swin solution to this connectivity problem is the shifted window scheme, the paper's most elegant contribution. In alternating transformer layers, the partition grid is cyclically shifted by (M/2, M/2) tokens before attention is computed. This means a token that sat at a window boundary in one layer finds itself well inside a new, different window in the next, and can now attend to tokens that were previously unreachable. Across two consecutive layers, every token has effectively been exposed to a larger neighborhood — larger than any single window — through two rounds of local attention from different origins. To implement this efficiently without padding artifacts at image boundaries, the shifted image is cyclically rolled so that displaced window pieces become contiguous in memory, and an additive attention mask of minus-infinity suppresses attention between tokens from non-adjacent regions in the original grid. The entire operation — the roll, the mask, the attention, the un-roll — is standard tensor arithmetic, implementable in a few lines of code.

The attention mechanism itself also differs from vanilla ViT in its handling of spatial position. Rather than adding absolute sinusoidal or learnable positional embeddings to token values, Swin incorporates a learnable relative position bias into the attention logit computation. Before each softmax, a learned scalar B(Δrow, Δcol) is added to the raw attention score between any two tokens, where Δrow and Δcol are their relative row and column offsets within the window. These offsets range over [-(M-1), M-1] in each dimension, so the bias table contains only (2M-1)² entries — for M=7, that is 169 scalars per head, a negligible parameter count. The relative parameterization is a natural inductive bias: proximity should matter more than absolute position, and the model need only learn how much to favor neighbors at each relative displacement. It also generalizes better across input sizes, since the relative offsets within a fixed-size window are the same regardless of whether the full image is 224×224 or 640×640.

The practical consequence of these design choices is that Swin can serve as a universal backbone across essentially all dense-prediction paradigms. For object detection, pairing Swin with an FPN and a standard two-stage or anchor-free detection head yields consistent improvements over matched-parameter ResNets and ResNeXts, reaching 58.7 AP on COCO val with Swin-L in its original form. For semantic segmentation on ADE20K, Swin combined with UperNet surpassed prior state-of-the-art methods by meaningful margins. For panoptic segmentation and instance segmentation, Mask2Former with a Swin-L backbone set multiple records on both COCO and ADE20K simultaneously. The model was designed for scale: a subsequent Swin V2 introduced post-normalization in residual blocks, a log-scale continuous relative position bias computed as the log of the absolute displacement, and a scaled cosine attention variant that replaced dot-product attention with cosine similarity, all aimed at stabilizing training at larger sizes. Swin-H at three billion parameters required these modifications to train without divergence, and demonstrated that hierarchical vision transformers could scale alongside language models.

Directly after Swin appeared, a challenge came from an unexpected direction. The same first author, Liu, published ConvNeXt as what amounted to a controlled experiment: start with a standard ResNet-50, apply Swin's training recipe — AdamW, cosine schedule, longer training, heavy augmentation — and then systematically introduce architectural choices one at a time. Larger per-stage kernel sizes (7×7 depthwise convolutions to simulate the window reception field), inverted bottleneck blocks, GELU activations instead of ReLU, fewer activation functions per block, LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm, and stage token ratios borrowed from Swin's (1:1:3:1 channel-to-block distribution). The resulting model, using entirely conventional convolutions, matched Swin on ImageNet, COCO, and ADE20K across multiple model sizes. The implication was pointed: much of Swin's advantage over older CNNs had come not from the attention mechanism itself but from training recipe improvements and architectural modernization. Attention was not obviously necessary for high-quality dense prediction, and convolutions were not obviously sufficient — both conclusions had to be qualified by the same caveat that the mechanisms interacted with scale, training compute, and data in ways that simple ablations could not fully characterize.

For your mental model, the right takeaway is not that one family dominates. When you reach for Swin rather than ViT, the reasons are concrete: linear complexity at the resolutions required by dense prediction, out-of-the-box multi-scale outputs that match the FPN convention, and pretraining stability that was demonstrated earlier and at larger scale than purely global transformers. When you reach for ConvNeXt, the reasons are equally concrete: simpler implementation with no cyclic shift or attention masking, slightly better efficiency at smaller model sizes, and strong accuracy without the engineering complexity. When you use Swin-L or Swin-H as a backbone in a segmentation or detection system, you are benefiting from all three of the original design decisions — hierarchy, windowed attention, shifted windows — working together, and you are participating in an architectural lineage that has since evolved into Swin V2, the CSWin Transformer, and the broader family of window-attention hierarchical models that remains active as of the time of writing.
