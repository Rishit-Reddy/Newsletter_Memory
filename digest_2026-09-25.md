# FlashAttention: IO-Aware Exact Attention, Tiling, and the Hardware-Algorithm Co-Design That Made Long-Context Transformers Practical

**Date:** 2026-09-25

---

When studying Vision Transformers, Swin, DETR, or the video transformers we examined recently, the theoretical story centers on query-key dot products, softmax normalization, and weighted value aggregation. The mathematical formulation fits cleanly on a single page. But running that computation on modern hardware reveals a structural problem that is orthogonal to floating-point operations entirely: the attention weight matrix, at N tokens, requires O(N²) memory to materialize, and writing that matrix back and forth between the GPU's main memory and its compute units consumes a disproportionate share of wall-clock training time. FlashAttention, introduced by Dao et al. in 2022, resolves this not by approximating attention but by restructuring how the computation is scheduled across the memory hierarchy — a contribution that is as much systems engineering as machine learning, and one that any serious practitioner of vision transformers needs to understand.

Modern GPUs organize memory in a two-level hierarchy that has profoundly different characteristics at each level. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) on the GPU card provides large capacity — tens of gigabytes on an A100 — but delivers data to the compute cores at roughly 2 TB/s. On-chip SRAM, by contrast, is tiny — approximately 20 MB distributed across all streaming multiprocessors on an A100 — but operates at speeds approaching 19 TB/s, nearly an order of magnitude faster. Standard attention implementations compute the full N×N logit matrix, write it to HBM, then read it back to apply softmax, write the normalized weights to HBM again, and read them once more to accumulate the weighted sum over values. For long sequences or high-resolution image tokens, this cascade of HBM reads and writes — not the multiply-accumulate operations themselves — becomes the dominant cost. The GPU's arithmetic units sit idle waiting for data to arrive from memory. In hardware profiling terms, attention is "memory-bandwidth-bound," not "compute-bound," and the standard textbook formulation has no concept of this distinction.

FlashAttention eliminates the intermediate HBM materialization through two interlocking ideas: tiled computation and the online softmax algorithm. Tiling divides the query, key, and value matrices into blocks small enough to fit simultaneously in SRAM. Computation proceeds block by block: for each tile of queries, the algorithm iterates over all tiles of keys and values, accumulating the output in on-chip registers. The difficulty is that softmax is a globally-coupled operation — normalizing a row of the attention matrix requires knowing all N dot products in that row before any output can be computed, which ordinarily forces you to materialize the entire row in HBM first. The online softmax algorithm, drawing on earlier numerical work, solves this by maintaining two running statistics per query: the current maximum logit seen so far, and the current sum of shifted exponentials. When a new key tile arrives and produces a new batch of logits, these running statistics are updated, and the previously accumulated output is retroactively rescaled by the ratio of old to new normalization constants. The update is algebraically exact: the final accumulated value is mathematically identical to what standard attention would have produced, not an approximation. At no point does an N×N matrix exist in memory.

The consequences are significant in both directions. Memory consumption for attention drops from O(N²) to O(N), since only the output accumulator and two running scalars per query row need to live in HBM at any time. More importantly, the number of HBM read and write operations falls from O(N²) to O(N·d) where d is the head dimension — and because attention is bandwidth-bound, this reduction translates almost linearly into wall-clock speedup. Dao et al. reported two to four times faster self-attention computation on representative sequence lengths, and training with FlashAttention on GPT-2 ran roughly three times faster end-to-end. For vision specifically, this changes the economics of high-resolution processing: a ViT processing 224×224 images at 16×16 patches generates 196 tokens, comfortably quadratic; at 8×8 patches the same image yields 784 tokens, and the quadratic cost of naive attention becomes prohibitive; FlashAttention makes 8×8-patch ViTs practical at reasonable batch sizes, directly opening the design space that architectures like ViT-22B and DINOv2's largest checkpoints explored.

FlashAttention-2, presented at ICLR 2024, revisited the parallelism strategy within the CUDA kernel. The original implementation parallelized across the batch and head dimensions, leaving each individual head to process the sequence dimension sequentially within a thread block. For small batch sizes — common during inference or in medical imaging workflows where scan volumes are large and batches of one or two are the norm — this left many GPU warps idle. FlashAttention-2 introduced parallelism across the sequence length dimension: different warps handle different query tiles independently, filling the GPU more efficiently. It also restructured the inner loop to reduce the fraction of operations that fall outside the tensor-core path, where the A100's FP16 matrix-multiply throughput is exceptional but other arithmetic is handled by ordinary CUDA cores at far lower throughput. These changes raised measured FLOP utilization from roughly 35% with the original FlashAttention to over 70%, approaching the ceiling imposed by memory bandwidth rather than compute. FlashAttention-3, developed for the H100's asynchronous WGMMA instructions and tensor memory accelerator, pushes this further by overlapping GEMM and softmax operations in a producer-consumer pipeline — the arithmetic and the data movement proceed concurrently rather than sequentially.

For the vision researcher, the most direct architectural consequence is not just speed but the relaxation of sequence-length constraints that shaped earlier design choices. Swin Transformer's shifted-window partitioning was motivated in part by the quadratic cost of global attention at high resolutions; with FlashAttention, global self-attention at moderate resolutions becomes tolerable even for billion-parameter models. Video transformers processing hundreds of frames — each frame itself decomposed into spatial tokens — depend on IO-aware kernels to avoid the catastrophic memory scaling that full spatiotemporal attention would otherwise imply. The MAE and DINOv2 pretraining runs we covered in earlier digests used memory-efficient attention as an engineering prerequisite; the architectures were designed assuming such kernels existed. Understanding FlashAttention is therefore not a detour into low-level systems engineering — it is a prerequisite for understanding why the vision architectures built since 2022 look the way they do, why certain patch sizes and sequence lengths appear repeatedly, and why the transition from research prototype to production training often depends as much on kernel engineering as on algorithmic novelty.
