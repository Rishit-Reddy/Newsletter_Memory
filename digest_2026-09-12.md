# 3D Gaussian Splatting: Explicit Scene Primitives, Adaptive Densification, and the Road to Real-Time Novel View Synthesis (arc 2/2)

*2026-09-12*

---

The first part of this arc traced how NeRF converts a scene into a continuous volumetric function — density and view-dependent radiance sampled at arbitrary 3D coordinates — and renders novel views by numerically integrating along rays through that function. The approach produces remarkably high-quality reconstructions, but it does so at a fundamental cost: every rendered pixel requires many evaluations of the MLP along its ray, and every evaluation involves a forward pass through a deep network. Optimized variants like Instant-NGP reduce training time from hours to seconds by replacing the MLP bottleneck with a multiresolution hash-encoded feature grid, and mip-NeRF 360 extends the formulation to unbounded outdoor scenes with principled anti-aliasing, but even the fastest implicit representations struggled to reach rendering rates above a handful of frames per second at full resolution. The core bottleneck is architectural: an implicit function must be queried, network evaluation is sequential within a sample, and the rendering integral requires many samples per ray. 3D Gaussian Splatting, introduced by Kerbl and colleagues in 2023, sidesteps this bottleneck not by making neural evaluation faster but by abandoning the implicit paradigm altogether in favor of an explicit, differentiable set of volumetric primitives that can be rasterized directly onto the image plane.

The scene representation in 3D Gaussian Splatting is a collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussians, each parameterized by a 3D mean (its center position), a full 3×3 covariance matrix controlling its shape and orientation, an opacity value, and a set of spherical harmonic coefficients encoding view-dependent color. A Gaussian with large eigenvalues along certain axes represents an elongated splat — useful for thin surfaces, foliage, or hair — while a nearly isotropic Gaussian approximates a soft volumetric blob. The choice of Gaussians as the primitive unit is not incidental: they are infinitely differentiable, project analytically onto the 2D image plane as 2D Gaussians, and compose under the volume rendering integral in a way that yields a clean alpha-compositing formula. Each 3D Gaussian projects onto the image plane by applying the camera's projective transform to its mean and propagating the 3D covariance through the Jacobian of the local affine approximation to the projective map; the resulting 2D covariance defines an ellipse over which the Gaussian contributes to nearby pixels. The rendered color of a pixel is then the ordered accumulation of contributions from all Gaussians whose projected extents overlap the pixel, composited front-to-back in depth order under a transmittance-weighted sum. This formula is structurally identical to the ray-marching integral used in NeRF — density-weighted radiance accumulated with transmittance — but it replaces per-ray network queries with a depth-sorted alpha-compositing pass over projected primitives.

The key to achieving real-time performance is tile-based rasterization. The image is divided into 16×16 pixel tiles, and all Gaussians whose projected 2D extents overlap a given tile are assigned to that tile's work queue. For each tile, the Gaussians in its queue are sorted by depth and rendered in a single pass through a GPU-parallel alpha-compositing kernel that touches each Gaussian at most once per pixel within the tile. This is structurally unlike the per-ray numerical integration in NeRF, which requires many sequential network evaluations per pixel; the tile-based rasterizer operates identically to a hardware graphics pipeline and on modern GPUs achieves rendering rates exceeding 100 frames per second at 1080p resolution. The acceleration over NeRF-based methods at equivalent quality is roughly two to three orders of magnitude, and unlike approaches that cache or bake a trained NeRF into a discretized representation, 3DGS achieves this without any post-training conversion step.

Training begins from a sparse point cloud produced by Structure from Motion preprocessing — typically COLMAP — which simultaneously provides initial Gaussian positions and camera calibration for the training views. Each point in the cloud seeds one Gaussian, initialized as a small isotropic primitive. The optimization then jointly minimizes photometric reconstruction loss between rendered training views and ground-truth images with respect to all Gaussian parameters: positions, covariances, opacities, and spherical harmonic coefficients. Because the 3×3 covariance matrix must remain positive semi-definite throughout, it is not parameterized directly; instead it is decomposed into a rotation quaternion and a scaling vector whose product gives the covariance, and gradients flow through this decomposition normally under automatic differentiation. The training signal is therefore the standard L1 and perceptual photometric loss, and the differentiability of the projection and compositing operations means that gradients propagate cleanly to all geometric and appearance parameters. In practice the optimization runs for around 30,000 iterations and converges in minutes on a modern GPU — a sharp contrast to the hours required by the original NeRF, and competitive even with Instant-NGP.

The most technically novel aspect of 3D Gaussian Splatting is its adaptive density control mechanism, which dynamically adjusts the number and spatial distribution of Gaussians during training to fit the scene's geometric complexity. Two failure modes must be addressed simultaneously. The first is under-reconstruction: in regions where the initial sparse point cloud provides no coverage, there are no Gaussians to model the geometry. This manifests as persistently high positional gradient magnitude on nearby Gaussians — the optimizer is attempting to move a primitive toward territory it cannot currently cover — and is resolved by cloning the offending Gaussian and displacing one copy along the gradient direction. The second failure mode is over-reconstruction: a single large-scale Gaussian attempting to cover geometry that requires finer subdivision will exhibit both high scale and high gradient. This is resolved by splitting the Gaussian into two smaller primitives positioned to tile the same spatial extent at finer resolution. Periodically during training, any Gaussian whose opacity falls below a threshold is pruned, since it contributes negligibly to rendered images while consuming memory. This adaptive densification loop allows the representation to grow from a sparse initial cloud of tens of thousands of Gaussians to millions of primitives concentrated where the scene demands fine detail, without any hand-specified resolution parameter. The result is a spatially adaptive explicit representation that inherits the efficiency of classical point-cloud and mesh rasterizers while retaining end-to-end trainability through a differentiable rendering loss.

The spherical harmonic representation for view-dependent color revisits a classical computer graphics technique in a learned context. Each Gaussian stores harmonic coefficients for each color channel; evaluating these coefficients at the current viewing direction gives the Gaussian's radiance toward that camera. Using only the zeroth-order term (a constant per channel) gives view-independent color, while including bands up to degree three allows the model to represent specular highlights and inter-reflections up to the bandwidth of those harmonics. This is more compact than NeRF's approach — where the full viewing direction is passed to a small MLP branch at every sample point — and admits direct closed-form evaluation during rasterization without any neural component.

The limitations of 3D Gaussian Splatting are as instructive as its strengths. The representation is memory-intensive: a scene of moderate complexity may require five to ten million Gaussians, each carrying dozens of floating-point parameters, resulting in several gigabytes of GPU memory. Unlike NeRF, which compresses scene information into the weights of a compact MLP, 3DGS stores explicit geometry and appearance per primitive and does not benefit from the parameter sharing that neural representations enjoy. The model also inherits structural difficulties from Gaussian primitives as a geometric approximation: sharp surface boundaries require many overlapping Gaussians arranged carefully near the boundary, and the representation tends to produce needle-like floater Gaussians in poorly observed or textureless regions. Surface extraction from a trained Gaussian cloud is non-trivial because the primitives are volumetric rather than surface-aligned, and subsequent work such as SuGaR introduces auxiliary surface-alignment regularization precisely to address this gap. From the curriculum's perspective, the arc from NeRF to 3DGS recapitulates a recurring tension in learned representation: implicit neural representations offer compactness, generalization pressure, and architectural flexibility at the cost of evaluation throughput, while explicit geometric representations offer fast inference and interpretable structure at the cost of memory and the need for differentiable approximations to traditionally discontinuous rendering operations. The past three years of literature have explored hybrids — neural feature fields backed by Gaussian primitives, Gaussian-based dynamic scene models using deformation fields analogous to those in VoxelMorph, and large-scale feedforward models that predict Gaussian parameters from a single image — and the emerging consensus is that neither paradigm is strictly dominant; the right choice depends on whether the bottleneck is rendering speed, storage budget, training data regime, or generalization across scenes.
