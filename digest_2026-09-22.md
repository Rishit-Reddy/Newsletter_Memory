# Multimodal Language Models: Connecting Vision Encoders to Language Models through Projection and Instruction Tuning

**Date:** 2026-09-22

The last several sessions have sketched an arc through the machinery of vision foundations: CLIP taught us that a contrastively trained image encoder can represent visual concepts in a space aligned with natural language, SAM showed how promptable segmentation scales when annotation is treated as a data-engineering problem, and the open-vocabulary detection work demonstrated that replacing a fixed classifier head with language embeddings unlocks generalization to arbitrary categories at test time. All of this concentrated effort on visual representation has a natural endpoint: models that do not merely embed images or localize objects, but hold a fluent conversation about visual content, answer questions, follow multi-step instructions, and reason over combinations of image and text. The multimodal language model (MLLM) line of work — Flamingo, LLaVA, InstructBLIP, and their descendants — is where those visual representations finally meet the expressive, contextual reasoning of autoregressive language models, and the design decisions made in connecting the two fundamentally shape what these systems can and cannot do.

The core architectural tension is that a vision encoder and a large language model (LLM) are trained independently, on different objectives, at different scales, and with representations that live in spaces that have no a priori geometric relationship. The vision encoder — typically a ViT trained with CLIP or a similar objective — produces a sequence of patch embeddings in some high-dimensional space. The LLM expects a sequence of token embeddings in its own learned space. Bridging these two requires either a learned projection that maps one space into the other, a cross-attention mechanism that allows the language model to selectively query the visual representation, or some combination, and each choice carries a different bias about how much information flows through, how it is compressed, and whether the visual representation remains frozen or is modified by the coupling procedure.

Flamingo, introduced by DeepMind in 2022, took the cross-attention route. The language model's transformer stack is interleaved with new cross-attention layers — called "perceiver resampler" blocks — that are inserted between the existing transformer layers. These new layers attend over the visual token sequence to inject visual context at multiple depths of the language model's processing. Crucially, the original language model weights are kept frozen and only the newly introduced cross-attention layers, along with a lightweight perceiver resampler that compresses the raw patch embeddings into a fixed number of visual tokens, are trained. This design choice reflects a principled decision about catastrophic forgetting: if you fine-tune the full LLM on image-text data, you risk degrading its language modeling capability; if you freeze it and train only the bridge, you preserve the LLM's priors and pay only in reduced flexibility. The perceiver resampler compresses a variable-length sequence of patch embeddings into a fixed pool of learned query vectors via cross-attention, effectively acting as a bottleneck that distills the visual signal into a compact representation before it reaches the language model. Flamingo demonstrated that this architecture supports genuine few-shot visual question answering: by including a small number of interleaved image-text examples in the context window, the model adapts to new visual tasks without any gradient updates, exploiting the in-context learning machinery already present in the LLM.

LLaVA (Large Language and Vision Assistant), proposed by Haotian Liu and colleagues in 2023, arrived at a strikingly simpler design with surprisingly strong results. Instead of cross-attention layers, LLaVA interposes a single learnable linear projection (later upgraded to a two-layer MLP) between the CLIP visual encoder and the LLM's input embedding space. The patch embeddings from the frozen CLIP encoder are projected into the LLM's token embedding space and then simply prepended to the text token sequence, treating visual tokens as if they were ordinary text tokens. This approach is almost absurdly minimalist: the "vision-language connector" is a single matrix multiplication, and the entire visual token sequence is handed to the LLM without any compression. What LLaVA discovered is that the bottleneck is not architectural sophistication in the connector — it is the quality of the instruction-following data used to train it. The training set consisted of GPT-4-generated conversations about images: given an image's caption and bounding box annotations, GPT-4 was prompted to hallucinate plausible conversation turns involving that image, producing data where a human asks questions about visual content and a detailed assistant response follows. This data is synthetically constructed yet semantically rich, and fine-tuning the connector and the LLM jointly on it produces a model that can engage in open-ended visual dialogue with far more coherence than simple captioning baselines. The LLaVA result suggested that the community had been over-engineering the vision-language interface and under-investing in curating instruction-following training signal.

InstructBLIP, also from 2023, explored a middle ground with a component called the Q-Former, inherited from BLIP-2. The Q-Former is a transformer that takes a fixed set of learned query vectors and allows them to attend over the visual patch embeddings via cross-attention, while simultaneously performing self-attention among the queries themselves. The output is a fixed-length sequence of query-derived visual tokens, which is then projected into the LLM's token space. This architecture acts as a trainable compression bottleneck: the query vectors learn, during training, which aspects of the visual content are most predictive of the instruction-following objective, and the resulting compressed representation adapts to the nature of the instruction being processed. InstructBLIP demonstrated that making the visual compression instruction-aware — feeding the text instruction into the Q-Former's self-attention so it can condition its queries on what is being asked — improves performance across a wide range of downstream tasks. When asked about color, the Q-Former attends differently than when asked about spatial relationships, and this dynamic routing produces more informative visual summaries than a frozen compression scheme can provide.

These three architectures collectively reveal a design spectrum. At one end sits Flamingo's deep cross-attention integration, which distributes visual information across the full depth of the LLM but requires training new parameters at every layer. In the middle sits InstructBLIP's Q-Former, which performs a single, learnable, instruction-conditioned compression before any LLM layer sees the visual signal. At the other end sits LLaVA's linear projection, which requires no architectural surgery and works because the CLIP visual space and the LLM's embedding space, both trained at scale on internet data, are already partially compatible. The practical verdict from ablation studies is that the LLM's inherent reasoning capacity and the quality of instruction tuning data dominate performance more than the connector architecture — a theme that recurs throughout this line of work and resonates with earlier findings in knowledge distillation, where the teacher's quality mattered more than the distillation mechanism's complexity.

The evaluation landscape for MLLMs introduced its own challenges. Standard VQA benchmarks, which require selecting from a finite set of answer candidates, fail to measure open-ended visual reasoning. Newer evaluations — MME, MMBench, LLaVA-Bench — present diverse tasks including scene understanding, counting, spatial reasoning, and document understanding, and these reveal that models trained purely on clean image-caption pairs generalize poorly to questions requiring fine-grained visual detail. A persistent failure mode is visual hallucination: the model generates plausible-sounding statements about visual content that simply isn't there, reflecting the LLM's strong prior over language co-occurrence. Mitigating hallucination requires careful data curation that includes negative examples and tasks that reward faithful description over fluent confabulation. This tension between fluency and faithfulness is one of the open problems that subsequent work like LLaVA-1.5, InternVL, and LLaMA-3-Vision continues to address.

Understanding MLLMs at a structural level is essential preparation for working with modern vision-language foundation models, because these systems increasingly serve as the default interface for medical image analysis, satellite image interpretation, and multimodal clinical reasoning — domains where distinguishing plausible outputs from faithful ones is not an academic concern but a patient safety one.
