# Neural Radiance Fields: Implicit Scene Representation, Volume Rendering, and the Frequency Problem in Coordinate Networks (arc 1/2)

*2026-09-11*

---

The past two weeks have traced a clean arc in generative vision: from contrastive and masked pretraining methods that build discriminative representations, through CLIP's alignment of image and language, to diffusion models that synthesize images by learning to reverse Gaussian corruption. Latent diffusion models in particular operate by compressing scenes into a continuous learned space and then performing iterative denoising within that latent manifold. A parallel strand of research, running somewhat independently since 2020 but now deeply intertwined with diffusion-based generation, asks a more constrained but foundational question: given a small set of 2D photographs of a scene, can a neural network learn a continuous volumetric model of that scene that renders correctly from any novel viewpoint? The answer, pioneered by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), is yes — and the mechanism by which NeRF achieves this changed the 3D vision community's understanding of what implicit representations can do.

The problem NeRF addresses is called novel view synthesis: predicting what a scene would look like from a camera position not present in the training set. Classical methods approached this either by reconstructing explicit 3D geometry (point clouds, meshes, voxel grids) from multi-view images and then rendering from that reconstruction, or by image-based rendering that interpolates between nearby training views. Both approaches are brittle in different ways. Explicit reconstructions require solving the ill-posed multi-view stereo problem, which degrades badly on thin structures, specular surfaces, and fine-grained texture. Image-based rendering sidesteps geometry but struggles outside the convex hull of observed viewpoints and fails to generalize to viewpoint changes that exceed the training distribution. NeRF's contribution is to represent the scene neither as an explicit surface nor as a lookup table of images, but as a continuous function mapping spatial coordinates to physical quantities — specifically, to volume density and view-dependent radiance — parameterized by an MLP.

To understand why this formulation is powerful, it helps to think carefully about what "rendering" means in the physics sense. Classical volume rendering, developed for scientific visualization in the 1980s and 1990s, models a scene as a participating medium: a volumetric field where at each point in space some fraction of incoming light is absorbed, emitted, or scattered. The light reaching a camera pixel along a ray cast from the camera through the scene is the integral over that ray of emitted radiance at each point, attenuated by the accumulated opacity of all intervening material. This transmittance-weighted integral has an analytic form that makes it amenable to Monte Carlo estimation: you sample points along the ray, evaluate density and color at each sample, and compute a weighted sum where the weights reflect how much of the scene each point actually contributes to the pixel given what lies in front of it. NeRF simply uses a neural network to produce the density and color values at each query point rather than reading them from a discretized voxel grid. The scene representation is entirely implicit — it lives in the weights of the MLP — and the rendering operation is fully differentiable, so the network can be optimized end-to-end by minimizing the photometric reconstruction loss between rendered pixels and the ground-truth images.

The MLP takes as input a 5D coordinate — a 3D spatial position and a 2D viewing direction expressed as a unit vector — and outputs volume density at that position plus an RGB color conditioned on the viewing direction. The asymmetry in treatment is intentional: density should depend only on position (it is a geometric property of the scene), while color is allowed to vary with viewing direction to model non-Lambertian effects such as specularity and iridescence. In practice the network is factored into two sub-networks: a deeper MLP consuming only the positional coordinate to produce density and an intermediate feature vector, followed by a shallower branch that concatenates the viewing direction and the feature vector to produce the final color. This design choice reflects a prior about scene structure — geometry is view-invariant, appearance is not — and it turns out to matter significantly for reconstruction quality on reflective surfaces.

The most technically surprising component of NeRF is positional encoding, and understanding it requires thinking about what MLPs are intrinsically biased toward. A vanilla MLP with smooth activations is a universal function approximator in theory, but in practice it imposes a strong implicit prior toward low-frequency solutions: the network naturally fits slowly varying functions far more easily than rapidly oscillating ones. This is not a failure of capacity but of inductive bias. A scene rendered at 800×600 pixels requires representing spatial frequencies on the order of individual pixels, which for a scene covering a meter of depth corresponds to millimeter-scale variation in density and color. An MLP consuming raw xyz coordinates cannot efficiently represent such high-frequency content because the input domain is too coarse relative to the required output bandwidth. NeRF's solution, borrowed from the Fourier feature networks literature, is to lift the input coordinates into a higher-dimensional space via a bank of sinusoidal functions at geometrically increasing frequencies: the coordinate x is mapped to the concatenation of sin(2^0 π x), cos(2^0 π x), sin(2^1 π x), cos(2^1 π x), …, sin(2^{L−1} π x), cos(2^{L−1} π x) for L frequency levels, and analogously for y, z, and the two viewing direction components. This encoding does not add information that was not already present, but it changes the effective bandwidth of the MLP's input, allowing the network to represent fine spatial detail without requiring implausibly deep or wide architectures.

A subtle design decision in the original NeRF paper is the use of two sets of sampled points per ray rather than one. A coarse network is first evaluated at a small set of uniformly sampled points along the ray; the resulting density estimates are used to build a piecewise-constant probability distribution over depth, concentrating weight near surfaces. A second set of additional samples is then drawn from this distribution — biased toward regions of high density — and both sets of points are fed to a separate fine network. The coarse-to-fine sampling strategy is important because the empty space between the camera and the nearest surface, and the empty space beyond the farthest surface, contribute nothing to the final render but dominate a naive uniform sample. Without hierarchical sampling, a prohibitively large number of network evaluations would be required to resolve the surfaces accurately.

The limitations of the original NeRF are instructive. Training a single scene from scratch requires hundreds of gradient steps over thousands of images and can take hours on modern hardware, making it impractical for real-time use. The representation is per-scene: a trained NeRF encodes exactly one environment and cannot generalize to novel scenes without retraining. View-dependent effects are modeled only approximately because the directional encoding uses a low-frequency spherical representation. And the original formulation assumes static scenes under constant illumination, collapsing in the presence of moving objects or changing light. Many subsequent papers — mip-NeRF addresses aliasing at different scales, NeRF-W handles per-image appearance variation in unconstrained internet photographs, Instant-NGP replaces the MLP bottleneck with a hash-encoded feature grid to accelerate training by orders of magnitude — can each be read as attacking one of these structural limitations while preserving the core volume-rendering formulation. Tomorrow's digest will pick up the story at the point where the field shifted from implicit MLP-based representations to explicit Gaussian primitives, examining how 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) achieves real-time rendering quality that NeRF-based methods could not match.
