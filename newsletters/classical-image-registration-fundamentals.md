# Classical Image Registration Fundamentals: Geometric Transformations, Similarity Measures, and Deformable Models

*Date: 2026-08-28*

---

The previous digest established the clinical and research landscape for image registration — why it is needed for adaptive radiotherapy, population neuroimaging, and multi-atlas label propagation, and why the problem resists easy solutions. We noted that classical optimization-based frameworks are principled but computationally expensive, and that learning-based methods later arose to amortize that cost at the price of introducing new questions about accuracy and topological validity. Before we can engage seriously with those trade-offs, we need to understand the classical machinery itself: what mathematical object the optimizer is searching over, how the quality of a candidate transformation is measured, and what structural properties the chosen representation must enforce. That is the subject of today's digest.

The problem statement is deceptively compact. Given a fixed image F : Ω → ℝ and a moving image M : Ω → ℝ, both defined over a spatial domain Ω ⊆ ℝ³, we seek a transformation T : Ω → ℝ³ such that the warped image M ∘ T is as similar to F as possible under some measure of image correspondence, subject to a regularizer that encodes prior beliefs about what biologically plausible transformations look like. Written as an objective, we minimize D(F, M ∘ T) + λ R(T) over T, where D is the dissimilarity metric, R is the regularization term, and λ trades them off. Every design decision in registration — the choice of transformation family, similarity measure, regularizer, and optimizer — is a choice about how to instantiate each of these terms, and the choices interact in non-trivial ways.

The transformation family is the first and most consequential choice, because it determines the expressiveness of the model and the geometry of the search space. At one end of the spectrum is the rigid transformation, which represents a pure rotation and translation: six parameters in three-dimensional space, encoding the motion of an idealized rigid body. Rigid registration is the appropriate model whenever the anatomical structure of interest is geometrically preserved across images — the same patient's bone between two scans, the skull relative to its contents in a single-session multi-modal acquisition, or intra-operative motion where the operative field is small and deformation is negligible. The full transformation is represented as a matrix in SE(3), the special Euclidean group, and because the parameter space is low-dimensional and the objective is typically smooth in this regime, gradient-based optimization is well-behaved. Affine registration extends the model by adding linear scaling and shearing, giving twelve parameters in 3D. The affine group GL(3) ⋉ ℝ³ contains rigid transformations as a subgroup, so affine registration is strictly more expressive, but crucially it remains a global model: the same linear map is applied to every voxel in the image. This is appropriate for compensating scanner-induced scaling differences between institutions, for approximate inter-subject brain alignment when fine anatomical correspondence is not needed, or as a preprocessing step that brings the images into rough global alignment before a subsequent deformable stage. Neither rigid nor affine registration can account for the spatially varying anatomical differences that distinguish one individual's brain from another — the particular pattern of gyral folding, the relative size of individual subcortical structures, the geometry of individual sulci. Those differences require a fundamentally more expressive representation.

Deformable registration replaces the global matrix with a dense displacement field u : Ω → ℝ³, so that the full transformation is T(x) = x + u(x) and u can vary continuously across the image domain. With one displacement vector per voxel, the representation has as many degrees of freedom as the image has voxels, which in a typical 256³ brain scan amounts to roughly sixteen million parameters. This is simultaneously the virtue and the problem of dense deformable registration: the model is expressive enough to capture any continuous deformation, but without constraints the search space is astronomically large, the problem is wildly ill-posed, and gradient descent will find pathological solutions in which the displacement field folds back on itself, tears tissue apart, or maps two distant voxels to the same location in the fixed space. The regularization term R(T) is the primary mechanism for excluding such solutions, and choosing it correctly is as important as any other modeling decision.

The dissimilarity metric D tells the optimizer how much improvement a candidate transformation provides, and the right choice depends almost entirely on the imaging modality. The simplest possible metric is the sum of squared differences, SSD = ∫_Ω (F(x) − M(T(x)))² dx, which assumes that corresponding anatomical structures have identical intensity values in both images. This assumption is reasonable for intra-session, single-modality registration where scan parameters are fixed, but it is sensitive to even moderate intensity non-uniformity from bias fields or varying tissue hydration, and it is wholly inappropriate when the images come from different modalities. Normalized cross-correlation (NCC) relaxes the requirement from intensity equality to a linear relationship: it measures the correlation between local image patches, making it insensitive to global multiplicative and additive intensity differences. NCC handles within-modality registration across slightly different acquisition protocols, but it too assumes a linear and globally consistent relationship between corresponding intensities, which fails across modalities.

The resolution to multi-modal registration came from information theory. Mutual information between two random variables X and Y measures the reduction in uncertainty about one variable when the other is observed: I(X; Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X, Y), where H denotes entropy. In the image registration context, F and M ∘ T are treated as jointly distributed random variables, with their joint histogram estimated from the voxel intensity pairs across the domain. Maximizing I(F; M ∘ T) with respect to T drives the optimizer toward transformations that make the intensity pairing between fixed and warped moving image as statistically predictable as possible — high mutual information means that knowing one image's intensity at a location substantially reduces uncertainty about the other's. The critical insight, developed independently by Maes et al. and by Viola and Wells in 1997, is that mutual information does not prescribe what the relationship between modalities should be. Bone appears bright in T1 MRI and as high Hounsfield values in CT; white matter appears bright in T1 and dark in T2. Mutual information does not need to know this mapping in advance — it merely requires that the mapping be consistent across the image. This generality is what makes it the dominant similarity measure in multi-modal registration to this day, though it has known failure modes when intensity distributions are highly non-stationary, as occurs near large pathological regions.

Addressing the deformable case, the dominant classical approach for representing the displacement field is the free-form deformation parameterized by B-splines, introduced for medical imaging by Rueckert et al. in 1999 in their work on non-rigid registration of breast MRI. The core idea is to place a coarse grid of control points Φ over the image domain, with uniform spacing δ in each spatial direction, and to represent the displacement at any point x as a weighted sum of the displacements of the nearest control points, where the weights are tensor products of cubic B-spline basis functions evaluated at the fractional offset of x within its grid cell. Formally, u(x) = ∑_{l,m,n} β³(x/δ − l) β³(y/δ − m) β³(z/δ − n) Φ_{l,m,n}, where β³ denotes the cubic B-spline kernel. Two properties of cubic B-splines make this parameterization particularly well-suited to deformable registration. First, cubic B-splines are C2-continuous, meaning the resulting displacement field has continuous second derivatives everywhere by construction. Smoothness is a structural guarantee of the representation, not a post-hoc penalty, and this is why B-spline FFD displacement fields have an inherently organic, tissue-like character even without regularization. Second, each cubic B-spline basis function has local support, spanning only four consecutive grid intervals in each spatial direction. Moving a single control point affects the displacement field only within a 4×4×4 grid cell neighborhood, leaving the displacement field elsewhere unchanged. This locality matches the physical intuition that registration failure near the liver should not force large corrections near the kidney or the spine. Optimization over the B-spline FFD reduces to gradient descent over the control-point displacements, with the gradient of D propagated back through the interpolation via the chain rule. The number of degrees of freedom grows as (N/δ)³, so the spacing δ directly controls the trade-off between representational expressiveness and computational cost.

Even with the B-spline parameterization imposing C2 smoothness, additional regularization is standard practice. The most common choice is bending energy, which penalizes the Frobenius norm of the Hessian of the displacement field integrated over the domain: R_bend(T) = ∫_Ω ∑_{i,j} (∂²u / ∂x_i ∂x_j)² dx. High bending energy corresponds to rapidly spatially varying displacements, and penalizing it biases the optimizer toward low-curvature, smoothly varying deformations. A complementary constraint, which becomes strictly necessary when the downstream application requires a bijective transformation, is the positivity of the Jacobian determinant: det(∇T(x)) > 0 everywhere on Ω ensures that the transformation is locally orientation-preserving and one-to-one. In practice this is enforced as a soft penalty that is activated whenever the Jacobian falls below a small positive threshold, though maintaining strict global positivity under unconstrained gradient descent requires careful step-size control and motivates the diffeomorphic parameterizations — stationary velocity fields and geodesic shooting — that we will encounter in the next digest.

A practical feature that cannot be omitted is the multi-resolution strategy. Direct optimization at full image resolution is vulnerable to local minima: at fine scales, small misalignments produce intensity differences that look similar to those produced by large misalignments, and gradient-based optimizers can easily converge to poor solutions. The standard remedy is to build a Gaussian image pyramid by progressively downsampling and smoothing both images, then optimize the registration at each level of the pyramid from coarsest to finest. At the coarsest level, high-frequency texture differences are suppressed, and only large-scale structural correspondences are visible; the optimizer captures the gross alignment. At each finer level, the transformation is initialized from the previous solution and the optimizer refines local correspondences. For B-spline FFD specifically, the control-point spacing is typically halved between levels, so the representation at each level is exactly twice as expressive as the previous one and the degrees of freedom expand in a principled manner. Together, the choice of transformation family, similarity metric, regularizer, and multi-resolution strategy constitute the complete classical registration pipeline, and the combinations that proved most reliable — affine initialization followed by B-spline FFD with NCC or mutual information, optimized on a multi-resolution pyramid with bending-energy regularization — remained state of the art for nearly two decades. Understanding why they work, and precisely where they fail, is the prerequisite for appreciating what deep learning methods actually contribute, which is the subject of the next digest.
