# Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning in Vision: SimCLR, MoCo, and the InfoNCE Objective

*2026-09-02 — arc 1/2*

---

The Chinchilla scaling laws underscore a fundamental constraint: you cannot scale a model without commensurately scaling its training data, and that data must be of sufficient quality to fill the model's capacity. But in many domains — clinical medical imaging chief among them — annotations are expensive, expert-dependent, and inherently limited. This asymmetry between raw data availability and label availability gave rise to a family of techniques collectively called self-supervised representation learning, where the supervisory signal is derived from the data itself rather than from human annotation. Through the period 2019–2021, contrastive learning emerged as the dominant paradigm within this family, producing visual representations that matched or exceeded supervised ImageNet pretraining in numerous downstream transfer settings. Understanding how contrastive methods work mechanically — what they optimize, where their gradients flow, and why they succeed — is prerequisite knowledge before confronting the masked autoencoder literature that eventually overtook them.

The foundational intuition in contrastive learning is deceptively simple: representations of two different views of the same image should be similar, while representations of views from different images should be dissimilar. A "view" here means a stochastic augmentation of the original image — random cropping, color jitter, Gaussian blurring, grayscale conversion, or some carefully designed composition of these. The augmentation policy is not incidental; it is the primary design choice that determines what invariances the learned representation will capture. If you apply aggressive color distortion as an augmentation, the resulting representations become invariant to color, which is useful for tasks where color is spurious but harmful for tasks where color is diagnostically meaningful, such as distinguishing tissue types in histopathology slides. This invariance-by-augmentation principle forces the engineer to reason carefully about which domain-specific transformations constitute genuine semantic-preserving changes, and this reasoning must precede, not follow, the choice of augmentation policy.

Chen et al. (2020) introduced SimCLR, which operationalizes the above idea through the NT-Xent (normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy) loss, a specific instantiation of the more general InfoNCE objective. Given a minibatch of N images, SimCLR generates 2N augmented views by applying two independently sampled stochastic augmentations to each original image. An encoder f — a ResNet-50 or larger — maps each view to a representation h, and a lightweight two-layer MLP projection head g maps h to a lower-dimensional space z where the contrastive loss is applied. The NT-Xent loss for a positive pair (z_i, z_j) is the negative log of the softmax over all 2(N-1) other examples in the batch treated as negatives. Mechanically, it asks the network to identify the true positive partner among a sea of negatives in the projected space. Two properties of this formulation are worth internalizing. First, the temperature hyperparameter τ controls the concentration of the distribution: a lower temperature makes the loss sharper, forcing the model to more aggressively separate non-matching pairs at the cost of noisier gradients for hard negatives that are genuinely semantically distinct. Second, the absolute batch size matters in a way that has no real analogue in supervised learning — larger batches provide more negatives per step, which strengthens the contrastive signal. SimCLR's best results required batch sizes in the thousands (4096–8192) distributed across TPUs, a significant practical barrier for researchers without large-scale compute.

Kaiming He et al. addressed the batch-size problem directly with MoCo (Momentum Contrast), which decouples the number of available negatives from the batch size through a dynamic dictionary implemented as a FIFO queue. MoCo maintains two encoders: a query encoder trained via gradient descent, and a key encoder whose weights are an exponential moving average of the query encoder's weights (θ_k ← m·θ_k + (1−m)·θ_q, with m typically 0.999). Queries are encoded by the query encoder; keys are encoded by the key encoder and stored in a queue of fixed size K, which can be set to 65,536 without increasing the per-step batch requirement. The InfoNCE loss compares each query against its single positive key (an augmented view of the same image encoded by the key encoder) and K−1 negative keys drawn from the queue. The slow-moving key encoder is the architectural innovation that makes this feasible: because the key encoder changes slowly relative to the queue length, keys in the queue were computed under parameters that are close to, though not identical with, the current key encoder. This approximate consistency is sufficient for the contrastive loss to remain meaningful. The momentum coefficient m is the critical sensitivity parameter: too low and the key encoder diverges rapidly from the query encoder, making old queue entries inconsistent; too high and the key encoder lags so far behind that it no longer reflects the model's evolving representational geometry. MoCo v2 incorporated the projection head from SimCLR and achieved competitive performance with substantially smaller batches, demonstrating that the ideas from the two frameworks were largely orthogonal and complementary.

The InfoNCE loss has a principled information-theoretic interpretation that clarifies both its power and its limitations. Maximizing InfoNCE approximately maximizes a lower bound on the mutual information I(z; z') between the representations of two views z and z'. The key insight is that what gets maximized is the information shared across both views — by design, this is the information that survives both augmentations, meaning it is the invariant semantic content of the image. If both augmentations preserve the object category while discarding exact pixel values, the representation converges toward an encoding of category-level identity. This explains both the success of contrastive methods on standard recognition benchmarks and their well-documented brittleness in settings where the "invariant" semantic content defined by the augmentation set does not align with the invariances relevant to the downstream task. In medical imaging, for instance, aggressive cropping can cause the representation to focus on texture rather than shape, which can be detrimental for pathology classification where both spatial context and shape carry diagnostic weight.

A subtle but reproducible finding shared across SimCLR, MoCo, and later work is that the contrastive objective should be applied in the projected space z rather than the representation space h that is actually used for downstream tasks. Consistently including the projection head improves linear evaluation accuracy on the backbone h by several percentage points. The most coherent interpretation is that the projection head absorbs the information distortion induced by the contrastive loss — specifically, the loss will suppress information that is not shared across views, and the projection head acts as a buffer that allows the backbone to retain such information. Without the projection head, the backbone itself must conform to the loss's implicit geometry, which forces it to discard potentially relevant signal.

Representation collapse — the trivial solution where every input is mapped to the same point in embedding space — is the central failure mode of contrastive learning, and negative pairs are its essential guard. The explicit repulsion between non-matching pairs imposes a diversity constraint that prevents constant-output solutions. This makes the role of negatives seem indispensable, which is why Grill et al. (2020) caused considerable surprise when they demonstrated BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent), a method using no negative pairs whatsoever that achieves competitive or superior representations. BYOL escapes collapse through a combination of a momentum encoder (borrowed from MoCo) and a stop-gradient operation on the target network, which together introduce an implicit regularization whose exact theoretical characterization remained an active area of debate. The existence of BYOL as a working method signals that the geometry of the contrastive objective is not the only path to high-quality representations, and it prefigures the masked autoencoder approach, where self-supervised signal comes from reconstruction rather than from contrastive pair discrimination — a shift we will examine carefully in the next installment.
