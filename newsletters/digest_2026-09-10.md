# Flow Matching and Rectified Flow: Continuous Normalizing Flows, Optimal Transport, and the Modern Generative Transport Framework

*Date: 2026-09-10*

The past two digests built up denoising diffusion probabilistic models from first principles and ended with latent diffusion — the architectural backbone of Stable Diffusion. A thread that ran through all of that was the idea of a trajectory: the forward process gradually corrupts data into noise, and the reverse process, learned by a neural network, recovers the data by traveling that trajectory in the opposite direction. Once you accept that framing, a natural question surfaces: is the noisy, variance-exploding stochastic path actually necessary, or was it a historical artifact of how score matching was first derived? Flow matching and rectified flow answer that question by constructing deterministic, straight-line trajectories between distributions and showing that learning to follow them is both simpler and empirically more efficient than learning to reverse a carefully crafted noise process.

The technical ancestor of flow matching is the continuous normalizing flow (CNF). A CNF parameterizes a time-dependent vector field v(x, t) such that integrating the ordinary differential equation dx/dt = v(x, t) from t = 0 to t = 1 transports every sample from a base distribution p_0 (typically a standard Gaussian) to a sample from the data distribution p_1. Because the ODE is invertible, you get both generation and exact log-likelihood for free — running the ODE forward generates data, running it backward maps data to a latent code. The catch, developed in Chen et al.'s Neural ODE work, is training: the standard maximum-likelihood objective requires computing the log-determinant of the Jacobian of the transformation, which via the instantaneous change-of-variables formula reduces to integrating the trace of the Jacobian over the trajectory. This trace integral must be computed by solving an adjoint ODE backward through time, and both the forward and backward solves require many neural function evaluations, making CNF training slow and numerically fragile at scale. FFJORD partially addressed this with a Hutchinson stochastic trace estimator, but the cost remained prohibitive at the model sizes needed for high-resolution image synthesis.

Flow matching sidesteps the likelihood objective entirely. Rather than asking the network to match the complex, implicitly defined marginal vector field that correctly transports the full distribution, the approach defines a set of per-sample conditional vector fields that are trivially computable in closed form and directly regresses against those. For a source sample x_0 drawn from p_0 and a target sample x_1 drawn from the data distribution p_1, the simplest conditional path is the straight line x_t = (1 - t) x_0 + t x_1, and the corresponding conditional vector field is the constant v(x_t | x_0, x_1) = x_1 - x_0. The flow matching objective minimizes the expected squared difference between the model's predicted field and this analytic conditional field over all times t uniformly sampled from [0, 1] and all pairs (x_0, x_1). The central theoretical result in Lipman et al. is that regressing on this tractable conditional objective is equivalent to regressing on the intractable marginal objective, because the marginal vector field is precisely the conditional expectation of the per-sample conditional fields with respect to all valid couplings of x_0 and x_1. This conditional-to-marginal reduction is what makes flow matching practical: you never need to compute or approximate the marginal field explicitly, yet you implicitly optimize the quantity that correctly transports the distribution.

The coupling between x_0 and x_1 turns out to have significant impact on the curvature of the learned trajectories and therefore on inference cost. If x_0 and x_1 are sampled independently from their respective distributions — an independent coupling — the straight-line paths between arbitrary pairs will often cross each other: a noise sample might be matched to a data point on the opposite side of the data manifold while a neighboring noise sample goes to a nearby data point. These crossings mean that no single straight-line vector field can correctly transport the marginal distribution, so the learned marginal field must curve to avoid contradictions, increasing the integration error when you discretize the ODE with a finite number of steps at inference. Optimal transport coupling eliminates crossings by construction: it matches each noise sample to the data sample that minimizes the total squared displacement over the entire coupling, producing paths that are globally non-crossing and individually as straight as possible. Training with an OT-conditioned flow matching objective yields vector fields that are close to linear along each trajectory, which in practice reduces the number of ODE solver steps required at inference to as few as ten to twenty-five, compared to the thousand-step DDPM schedule or the fifty-to-two-hundred step DDIM schedule covered in yesterday's digest.

Rectified flow, developed concurrently by Liu et al., reaches a structurally identical formulation from a different starting point. Their central observation is that straight-line flows are optimal in a transport cost sense under mild conditions, and they ask whether any given coupling — including the independent one — can be progressively straightened through a self-supervised procedure. The approach is iterative: first train a flow model on the independent coupling using the same conditional regression objective as flow matching, then generate new paired samples by running the trained model forward from noise to approximate data, and retrain the flow on this induced coupling, which is straighter because the model's own trajectories have already partially organized the transport plan. Repeating this reflow procedure progressively reduces the curvature of the learned trajectories until they can be distilled into a one-step generator that approximates the full ODE solution with a single network evaluation. This distillation path connects rectified flow to consistency models and represents one of the primary routes to single-step generation without adversarial training objectives.

A subtlety worth making explicit is that flow matching and diffusion are not as distinct as they might appear from the outside. Both frameworks learn a vector field that transports a simple base distribution to the data distribution; diffusion arrives at this field indirectly through a specific noise schedule and score matching, while flow matching parameterizes the field directly and learns it via conditional regression. The connection is precise: for any diffusion process governed by an Itô SDE, there exists a corresponding probability flow ODE with the same marginal distributions at every time t, and the drift of that ODE is determined by the score function through the Fokker-Planck equation. This means that a DDIM sampler is already traversing a deterministic flow, and its trajectories can be reinterpreted as those of a continuous normalizing flow. What flow matching contributes is the realization that you can learn this flow directly without going through the stochastic process at all, and that linear conditional paths provide a particularly clean regression target that scales well with model capacity.

The practical impact has been substantial. Stable Diffusion 3 and the FLUX family of image models use multimodal diffusion transformer architectures trained with flow matching objectives, replacing the noise schedule and denoising parameterization of earlier LDM architectures with direct velocity prediction on linear interpolation paths. The visual quality improves, the inference step count drops, and the text conditioning is integrated through joint attention over interleaved image and text token sequences in the same transformer blocks — a departure from the cross-attention conditioning used in the UNet backbone of Stable Diffusion 1 and 2. The latent space remains the same frozen VAE, so the representation layer is unchanged; the gains come entirely from the cleaner transport objective and the architectural changes in the denoising network itself.
