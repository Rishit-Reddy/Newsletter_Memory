# Object Detection Lineage Part II: One-Stage Detectors and DETR's Set-Prediction Paradigm

**Date:** 2026-08-31

---

Yesterday's digest traced the two-stage detection lineage from R-CNN through Faster R-CNN, arriving at a system where a Region Proposal Network shares convolutional features with a classification head to propose and then refine candidate bounding boxes. The two-stage design achieves high accuracy but carries an inherent sequential cost: proposals must exist before they can be classified. The obvious question this raises is whether the two stages can be collapsed into one without sacrificing the accuracy advantages that made Faster R-CNN attractive.

The single-shot detector (SSD, Liu et al., 2016) demonstrated that dense prediction across a regular grid could work, but one-stage methods consistently underperformed two-stage systems on benchmarks like COCO. The reason, diagnosed clearly by Lin et al. in the RetinaNet paper (ICCV 2017), is not architectural inadequacy but a training pathology. In any image, the vast majority of grid locations and candidate boxes contain background. In a two-stage system, the region proposal step already filters candidates to a manageable set; the second-stage classifier never sees the full avalanche of easy negatives. In a one-stage system, every position on the feature map is classified at every scale, meaning a single forward pass may evaluate tens of thousands of anchor locations, of which only a handful correspond to true objects.

Standard cross-entropy loss does not discriminate between easy and hard examples. When a well-trained model assigns a background location a probability of 0.999 to be background, that example contributes a loss of roughly −log(0.999) ≈ 0.001, which individually is tiny but collectively — summed over tens of thousands of easy negatives — dominates the gradient signal and overwhelms the small number of object-positive examples that actually require learning. The model learns to suppress backgrounds aggressively, but the hard positives and hard negatives that matter for detection quality receive progressively less gradient attention. Lin et al. propose a surprisingly simple fix: multiply the cross-entropy loss by a modulating factor that depends on how well the model already predicts the correct class. Formally, if p_t denotes the probability the model assigns to the correct label, focal loss is −(1 − p_t)^γ · log(p_t), where γ is a focusing parameter typically set to 2. When p_t is large (the model is confident and correct), the factor (1 − p_t)^γ is close to zero, sharply reducing that example's contribution. When p_t is small (the model is uncertain or wrong), the factor approaches 1 and the loss closely matches standard cross-entropy. The effect is to re-weight the training distribution toward examples the model finds difficult. This modification alone closes the gap between one-stage and two-stage detectors, and in many configurations surpasses them.

The RetinaNet architecture pairs focal loss with a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) backbone. FPN constructs multi-scale feature representations by combining a standard bottom-up convolutional backbone with a top-down pathway and lateral connections: high-resolution, semantically weak features from early layers are fused with low-resolution, semantically rich features from later layers, yielding a pyramid in which each level has both spatial detail and semantic content. RetinaNet attaches two lightweight fully-convolutional subnetworks at each FPN level — one for classification and one for box regression — predicting over a dense tiling of anchor boxes with multiple aspect ratios and scales. Because the two subnetworks do not share weights, the classification and regression tasks can specialize independently, which was found empirically to improve accuracy over sharing a common head.

RetinaNet still requires careful anchor design: choosing the right anchor sizes, aspect ratios, and matching thresholds is a hyperparameter burden that affects final accuracy. FCOS (Tian et al., ICCV 2019) eliminates anchors entirely by treating each spatial location as a potential object center and having the network predict how far that location lies from each edge of the enclosing ground-truth box. For a location (x, y) inside a ground-truth box, the regression targets are the four distances to the left, right, top, and bottom edges; the network predicts these four scalars rather than offsets relative to a predefined anchor. Because regression targets are pure distances without anchor priors, FCOS works across all scales without anchor enumeration. This creates an ambiguity when two objects overlap — the same spatial location may lie inside multiple ground-truth boxes, with no anchor matching rule to resolve the conflict. FCOS resolves this by assigning objects to FPN levels based on size: small objects are handled at high-resolution early levels and large objects at low-resolution late levels, a design that mirrors the intuition behind FPN itself. A residual ambiguity remains when objects of similar size overlap, handled by preferring the smaller enclosing box.

Additionally, locations far from the geometric center of an object produce low-quality detections. FCOS introduces a centerness branch that predicts, for each location, a score measuring how close the location is to the object center, computed as the geometric mean of the ratios min(l,r)/max(l,r) and min(t,b)/max(t,b). During inference, classification scores are multiplied by centerness to suppress peripheral detections without the need for a learned objectness head. The centerness formulation is clean and parameter-free: it requires no additional labels beyond the bounding box annotations already present.

Both RetinaNet and FCOS, despite their differences, share a fundamental post-processing step: non-maximum suppression (NMS). After the network produces scores for thousands of candidate locations, NMS greedily removes boxes with high spatial overlap with any higher-scoring box, under the assumption that those represent duplicate detections. This heuristic introduces hyperparameters — overlap threshold, score threshold — and can fail when objects genuinely overlap at high IoU, as in a crowd scene. Carion et al. (ECCV 2020) ask whether object detection can be reframed entirely so that NMS is never needed.

Their answer is to treat detection as direct set prediction. The ground truth for an image is a set of (class, bounding box) pairs; the network should predict this set in a single forward pass without any sequential filtering. A set, unlike an ordered list, has no canonical ordering, which makes designing the training loss non-trivial. If the model outputs N predictions and there are M ground-truth objects (with M < N), one cannot simply compare the i-th prediction to the i-th object — there is no meaningful ordering. Instead, DETR finds the optimal one-to-one matching between the N predictions and the M objects plus (N − M) "no object" tokens, using the Hungarian algorithm applied to a cost matrix that combines classification scores and bounding box costs — an L1 loss on normalized box coordinates plus a scale-invariant generalized IoU term that penalizes non-overlapping pairs more harshly than the standard IoU would. Hungarian matching runs in cubic time in the number of predictions but N is small (100 in the original implementation) and this step runs on CPU outside the main gradient computation, adding negligible wall-clock overhead.

Once the matching is determined, the set prediction loss penalizes each matched (prediction, ground-truth) pair with standard cross-entropy and bounding box regression losses, and penalizes each unmatched prediction with a small cross-entropy cost for predicting any class other than "no object." Because the matching is one-to-one by construction, there are no duplicate detections by design, and NMS is genuinely unnecessary. The generalized IoU term in the matching cost matters for a subtle reason: L1 distance between box coordinates is not scale-invariant, meaning a small error on a large box contributes far less than the same absolute error on a small box. GIoU normalizes this by the union of the two boxes, making the cost comparable across scales.

The DETR architecture passes a CNN backbone's feature map through a standard transformer encoder-decoder. The encoder allows every spatial position to attend to every other, building a global representation of the image before detection — a sharp contrast to convolutional detectors, where the receptive field at any given layer is determined by the depth and dilation of preceding convolutions. The decoder receives N learned positional embeddings, called object queries, which are added to a content embedding initialized to zero. Through alternating self-attention (queries attend to each other, which encourages diversity and suppresses near-duplicate outputs) and cross-attention (queries attend to the encoder output, reading global image information), each query learns to decode one particular object from the image. A shared feed-forward network applied independently to each query produces the final class distribution and normalized box coordinates.

DETR's training dynamics are qualitatively different from those of convolutional detectors. Because object queries are initialized randomly and the optimal matching is recomputed at every training step by the Hungarian algorithm, queries take many iterations to specialize — converging slowly toward detecting large objects, small ones, or objects in particular spatial regions. The original paper trained for 500 epochs on COCO to match Faster R-CNN's performance, compared to roughly 36 epochs for a well-tuned Faster R-CNN. Deformable DETR (Zhu et al., 2021) addresses this convergence issue by replacing full cross-attention with deformable attention: each query, instead of attending to all spatial positions uniformly, attends to a small learned set of sampling points weighted by predicted attention weights. This drastically reduces the quadratic memory and computation cost of the original attention layers, accelerates convergence to around 50 epochs, and also improves small-object detection, where the original global attention provides insufficient spatial resolution.

The conceptual contribution of DETR is more significant than its benchmark numbers at launch: it demonstrated that a well-posed combinatorial loss formulation can eliminate hand-designed post-processing entirely, and it opened a research lineage — Deformable DETR, Conditional DETR, DAB-DETR, DN-DETR, DINO-DETR — in which each successive work either accelerates convergence by providing better initial query positions, introduces auxiliary denoising tasks during training to stabilize the Hungarian matching, or refines the attention mechanisms. Within two years of DETR's publication, transformer-based detectors unambiguously outperformed purely convolutional systems on standard benchmarks. The arc from R-CNN through Faster R-CNN, RetinaNet, FCOS, and DETR traces not just a chronological sequence of architectures but a coherent replacement of hand-designed inductive biases — sliding windows, predefined anchors, greedy suppression heuristics — with learned structures guided by cleaner loss formulations, each step isolating and solving one remaining failure mode of its predecessor.
