# Learning-Based Image Registration: VoxelMorph, Diffeomorphic Deformations, and End-to-End Training with Spatial Transformer Networks

**Date:** 2026-08-29

---

Yesterday we traced registration back to its mathematical foundations: the rigid and affine families, the progression to B-spline free-form deformations, and the three families of similarity measure — sum of squared differences, normalized cross-correlation, and mutual information. The unifying computational pattern was iterative numerical optimization: begin with an initial alignment, evaluate a similarity measure between fixed and warped moving images, compute analytical or numerical gradients with respect to the transformation parameters, and descend until convergence. For clinical-resolution 3-D volumes this procedure can take many minutes or hours, and it requires fresh optimization for every new image pair because nothing learned on past pairs is retained. The natural question, which the medical imaging community began asking seriously around 2017, is whether a neural network could absorb the registration problem during training and then produce deformation fields at inference time in a single forward pass.

The enabling primitive arrived two years earlier, in a different context entirely. Jaderberg and colleagues introduced the Spatial Transformer Network (STN) as a differentiable module that can spatially transform a feature map under the control of a localization sub-network. The module consists of three parts: a small network that predicts transformation parameters from the input, a grid generator that uses those parameters to produce a sampling grid of spatial coordinates in the input frame, and a differentiable sampler that reads off pixel values at those coordinates using bilinear interpolation. Bilinear interpolation is the key — it is piecewise-differentiable almost everywhere, so gradients flow back from the output image through the sampling coordinates and onward to the localization network. Before the STN, inserting a spatial transformation anywhere in a training pipeline broke the gradient chain; after it, spatial resampling became just another differentiable layer. The STN paper demonstrated this for a narrowly parameterized family of global affine transforms on 2-D images, but the geometry extends without modification to arbitrary dense displacement fields in 3-D, which is exactly the setting medical image registration requires.

VoxelMorph, introduced by Balakrishnan and colleagues, is the first large-scale demonstration that this extension works in practice. The architecture is a U-Net variant whose input is the concatenation of the fixed and moving image volumes along the channel axis, and whose output is a dense displacement field of the same spatial resolution as the input. This field is passed to an STN-style differentiable sampler, which warps the moving image to produce a predicted fixed image. Training is entirely unsupervised in the sense that no ground-truth deformation fields are required. The loss function has two terms: a normalized cross-correlation between the warped moving image and the fixed image, which drives the network to find voxel correspondences, and an L2 norm on the spatial gradient of the predicted displacement field, which discourages spatially rough deformations. Crucially, because the entire pipeline — encoder-decoder, displacement field, STN sampler, and loss — is assembled from differentiable operations, standard stochastic gradient descent can be applied directly. The network trains on a population of image pairs drawn from a dataset (brain MRI atlases were the original domain), learns the statistical structure of anatomically plausible deformations, and generalizes to unseen pairs at inference time in roughly a second on a modern GPU, compared with many minutes for classical iterative solvers run to comparable accuracy.

Plain displacement fields, however, carry a topological risk that matters enormously for clinical applications. Nothing in the loss prevents the predicted field from being non-injective: two source voxels may be mapped to the same target location, or the local Jacobian of the transformation may become negative, indicating a fold in the deformation. Folded deformations are anatomically implausible — tissue volumes cannot interpenetrate — and they preclude computing the inverse transformation, which is often needed for propagating labels or atlases in both directions. The mathematical object that avoids all of this is a diffeomorphism: a smooth, bijective mapping whose inverse is also smooth. Diffeomorphisms preserve orientation and topology by construction. Classical solvers such as SyN, embedded in the ANTs toolkit, and DARTEL enforce diffeomorphism via the theory of geodesics on the group of diffeomorphisms, at considerable computational expense. Extending a learning-based framework to produce diffeomorphic output without giving up the single-pass inference speed was the central problem Dalca and collaborators addressed in the follow-up to VoxelMorph.

Their solution rests on the relationship between diffeomorphisms and velocity fields. For any smooth, time-independent velocity field v, the ordinary differential equation dφ/dt = v ∘ φ, integrated from t = 0 to t = 1, produces a diffeomorphism φ. The integration can be computed numerically via the scaling-and-squaring algorithm: recognize that exp(v) = exp(v / 2^T)^{2^T}, and approximate exp(v / 2^T) ≈ id + v / 2^T for large T; then compose this small-displacement approximation with itself 2^T times using successive squarings, requiring only T forward composition steps. Each composition is itself a spatial resampling, so the entire procedure is differentiable end to end. The modified network predicts a stationary velocity field rather than a displacement field directly, and the scaling-and-squaring integration sits as a fixed differentiable layer between the network output and the STN sampler. The resulting deformation is guaranteed to be a diffeomorphism whenever the velocity field is sufficiently smooth — a condition encouraged by the gradient regularizer on v. The same Dalca et al. paper frames the problem within a probabilistic graphical model where the deformation field is a latent variable. The network serves as an amortized variational encoder: its output parameterizes a Gaussian approximation to the posterior over deformations given the image pair. Optimizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO) yields the same reconstruction and regularization terms as the deterministic formulation, but the KL divergence interpretation makes the regularization weight principled rather than an arbitrary hyperparameter.

Several practical design choices recur across the field and are worth understanding precisely. For intra-modal registration — brain MRI to brain MRI, or lung CT to lung CT — normalized cross-correlation is the preferred similarity measure because it is insensitive to linear intensity rescaling, which varies across scanners and acquisition sessions. For multi-modal registration — CT to MRI, or PET to MRI — NCC is inadequate because the functional relationship between intensities in the two modalities is non-linear; mutual information estimated from joint intensity histograms is the standard alternative, though its gradient is expensive to compute exactly and approximations via Parzen window estimation are commonly used. When anatomical labels are available for a subset of training pairs, a segmentation-guided Dice loss on warped segmentations can be added as auxiliary supervision, dramatically improving biologically meaningful alignment in regions where image contrast alone is insufficient to drive correspondence. This semi-supervised variant consistently outperforms purely intensity-driven training on held-out label-overlap metrics, at the cost of requiring some annotated data.

What the VoxelMorph family of methods ultimately established is that the registration problem is tractable with standard deep learning tools when the architecture and loss are chosen appropriately. The U-Net encoder-decoder is well matched to registration for the same reason it is well matched to segmentation: its multi-scale skip connections allow the network to capture both coarse global alignment and fine local deformation structure, mirroring the coarse-to-fine pyramid strategies that classical solvers use. The critical conceptual advance over earlier attempts at learned registration was recognizing that unsupervised training through a differentiable warp — rather than supervised regression toward pseudo-ground-truth displacement fields produced by a classical solver — removes the classical solver from the loop entirely and avoids inheriting its errors. The accuracy of learned registration is now competitive with SyN-level classical solvers on standard benchmarks, at inference times that are orders of magnitude lower, which opens pathways to intraoperative use and large-cohort population studies that were previously computationally prohibitive.
