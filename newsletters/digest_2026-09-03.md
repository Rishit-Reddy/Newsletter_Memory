# Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning in Vision Part II: Non-Contrastive Methods, Collapse Prevention, and the Emergence of DINO

**Date:** 2026-09-03

---

Yesterday we established how SimCLR and MoCo frame visual representation learning as an instance discrimination problem, training a network under the InfoNCE objective to attract augmented views of the same image while repelling views drawn from different images. A useful way to understand what that objective actually enforces comes from Wang and Isola's geometric decomposition: the InfoNCE loss, when written carefully, separates into an alignment term that pulls matched pairs toward the same point on the unit hypersphere, and a uniformity term that distributes all embeddings as evenly as possible across the sphere's surface. The uniformity term is precisely what the negative examples provide. Without repulsion, the trivially optimal solution is for the encoder to map every input to a single constant vector, perfectly satisfying alignment at the cost of representing nothing at all. This representational collapse has no gradient signal to escape from, because every example looks identical. Once this framing was clear, the field's central question became whether negatives are the only mechanism that prevents collapse, or whether other structural choices could substitute for them.

Grill and colleagues answered this in 2020 with BYOL — Bootstrap Your Own Latent — which discarded negative pairs entirely. BYOL maintains two networks: an online network and a target network. The online network, equipped with an additional small prediction MLP head attached above the projector, is trained to predict the target network's representation of a differently-augmented view of the same image. The key asymmetry is that the target network's weights are not updated by gradient descent; instead they track an exponential moving average of the online network's weights, lagging behind it. The online network chases a slowly-moving target, and the target network continuously incorporates the online network's progress with a delay. When ablations removed either the predictor head or the momentum averaging and kept only the other, collapse ensued immediately. Both together proved sufficient for stable training, and BYOL matched or exceeded the linear probe accuracy of SimCLR across standard benchmarks. At the time, no theoretical explanation fully accounted for why this worked, and several groups published analyses claiming it should collapse before empirically verifying it did not. The practical upshot was significant: BYOL removed the dependency on large batch sizes needed to populate the negative pool, bringing competitive self-supervised learning within reach of smaller compute budgets.

Chen and He then showed with SimSiam (CVPR 2021) that even the momentum encoder is unnecessary. Their setup is remarkably minimal: two augmented views of an image are passed through the same encoder and projector, but the loss is a cosine similarity between a predictor applied to one view's representation and a stop-gradient copy of the other view's representation. The stop-gradient operation is the entire collapse-prevention mechanism — without it, both branches receive symmetric gradients and the network quickly collapses. Chen and He's theoretical account frames SimSiam as an implicit expectation-maximization procedure. The stop-gradient branch acts as a pseudo-label or cluster centroid estimate, frozen momentarily while the gradient branch optimizes the representation toward it; on the next step, the roles implicitly swap. This EM lens removes the need to appeal to negative repulsion at all and suggests that self-supervised learning can be understood as iterative clustering without explicit cluster assignments. The theoretical picture remains an approximation, but the SimSiam paper's ablations are among the cleaner pieces of empirical evidence that stop-gradient and the predictor head together are mechanistically sufficient.

A complementary objective function appeared in Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021), which drew on Barlow's 1961 neuroscience hypothesis that the goal of sensory processing is to produce a factorial code — a representation in which each component is statistically independent and captures a distinct feature of the input. Applied to self-supervised learning, Barlow Twins forms a cross-correlation matrix between the representation vectors of two augmented views of a batch, computed after batch normalization along the batch dimension. The loss penalizes diagonal entries for deviating from one, encouraging each feature dimension to have maximal agreement across views, while simultaneously penalizing off-diagonal entries for being nonzero, discouraging any two dimensions from co-varying across views. The result is a representation where each dimension independently encodes a distinct transformation-invariant property of the input. Unlike the hypersphere uniformity of contrastive methods, Barlow Twins' uniformity is enforced through the off-diagonal decorrelation, and neither memory banks nor large batches are required. The method's connection to prior information-theoretic ideas about redundancy reduction gave it a principled aesthetic that distinguished it from what might otherwise have looked like an ad hoc variant of existing approaches.

DINO, introduced by Caron and colleagues at ICCV 2021, brought these threads together with a self-distillation framework built specifically around vision transformers, and made an empirical observation that reframed how the community thought about what these models learn. DINO uses a student-teacher architecture similar to BYOL's, with a momentum teacher, but its output space is a set of prototype scores — a categorical distribution over learned cluster centers. The student is trained to match the teacher's output distribution for differently-augmented views of the same image via a cross-entropy loss, while two mechanisms prevent collapse: centering subtracts a running mean of teacher outputs, preventing any single prototype from dominating; sharpening applies a low temperature to the teacher's softmax, producing peaked distributions that carry strong learning signal. The empirical discovery that made DINO widely discussed was that the self-attention maps of DINO-trained ViT-S models spontaneously segmented objects from backgrounds with striking precision, despite receiving no segmentation supervision. In supervised ViTs and in convolutional self-supervised models, this property was largely absent. The interpretation is that DINO's objective — enforcing consistency of a softmax distribution across strongly-different crops of the same image — forces the transformer's attention heads to identify which image regions remain semantically stable across crops, which in practice corresponds to object boundaries and salient regions. The attention maps effectively function as unsupervised foreground detectors.

Stepping back across these four methods, a unifying principle becomes visible. Whether the objective is contrastive repulsion, bootstrap self-consistency, decorrelation, or self-distillation, every approach encodes the same implicit assumption: the augmentation policy specifies which image variations are semantically irrelevant, and the training objective rewards the encoder for ignoring those variations while preserving any information that distinguishes images from each other. The choice of augmentation policy is therefore not just an engineering detail; it is the mechanism by which domain knowledge enters the representation learning process. In medical imaging, for example, color jitter may be irrelevant but spatial orientation may carry diagnostic meaning, so standard augmentation policies derived from natural-image benchmarks can degrade transfer. This has motivated domain-specific contrastive pre-training protocols — for histopathology, radiology, and retinal imaging — where augmentation strategies are designed around the target distribution's statistics. The next major departure in visual self-supervised learning moved away from augmentation-invariance entirely: masked image modeling, exemplified by the Masked Autoencoder, frames pre-training as a reconstruction problem, removing the need to specify semantic invariances through augmentation design and instead deriving learning signal from the statistics of natural image patches themselves.
