# Knowledge Distillation in Deep Vision Models: Temperature Scaling, Feature Mimicking, and the Relational Transfer Perspective

*2026-09-17*

Over recent weeks this digest has traced a curriculum from foundational encoder–decoder architectures like U-Net through to large-scale vision transformers, self-supervised pretraining schemes, and the video-temporal generalizations developed in yesterday's entry. A thread running quietly through all of that material is the practical question of deployment: once a large model has learned good representations, how do you obtain a smaller model that preserves most of the predictive quality while meeting memory and latency constraints? Knowledge distillation is the principal answer the research community has developed, and it connects backward to nearly every architecture we have examined.

The original formulation due to Hinton, Vinyals, and Dean considers a classification setting in which a large, possibly ensembled "teacher" model has already been trained and produces a soft distribution over classes for any given input. A small "student" model is then trained not on one-hot labels alone but on a weighted combination of the ordinary cross-entropy loss and a term that minimizes the KL divergence between the student's output distribution and the teacher's output distribution. The crucial insight is that soft probabilities carry structural information absent from one-hot targets: a teacher that assigns probability 0.05 to "husky" while assigning 0.70 to "wolf" encodes the fact that these two classes share visual texture, fur, and body morphology in ways that a hard label discards entirely. A temperature parameter τ rescales both logits before softmax, producing softer distributions that amplify these inter-class relationships. At τ = 1 the teacher distribution collapses back toward near-argmax behavior for confident predictions; as τ grows toward 4 or 8 the distribution spreads, giving the student much stronger gradient signal from what Hinton called the "dark knowledge" embedded in the teacher's off-peak probabilities. The student trained on this augmented objective converges to better generalization than training on hard labels alone, often approaching the teacher's accuracy at a fraction of the parameter count.

The logit-matching view works well for classification but runs into a conceptual limit when you want to transfer representations across architectures that differ substantially in depth or width: if the teacher's penultimate layer has 2048 dimensions and the student's has 256, there is no direct surface to align without an adapter. Feature-level distillation methods address this by inserting a thin projection so that intermediate feature tensors can be matched directly. FitNets, the earliest clean articulation of this idea, trains the student to reconstruct the teacher's hidden representations at a chosen guide layer using a regressor, and optionally freezes those layers before continuing with the task loss. Later work found that which layer to hint matters considerably: aligning attention maps rather than raw activation volumes transfers more of the teacher's spatial reasoning behavior, a property especially valuable in dense prediction tasks like semantic segmentation where feature maps at intermediate resolution directly govern boundary quality. For U-Net-style segmentation students, distilling skip-connection features from the teacher has been shown to improve boundary precision compared to distilling only the bottleneck, precisely because those skip connections carry the high-frequency spatial detail the decoder depends on.

Relational knowledge distillation takes a different philosophical stance. Rather than asking the student to mimic the teacher's absolute representations, it asks whether the student's internal geometry preserves the same pairwise and triplet-level structure that the teacher's geometry exhibits. Park, Kim, Lu, and Cho define a distance-wise loss that penalizes differences in pairwise Euclidean distances between sample embeddings in teacher and student space, and an angle-wise loss that penalizes differences in signed triplet angles. Because these losses operate on ratios and differences between embedding positions, they are invariant to the absolute scale and orientation of the space, making them applicable when teacher and student are architecturally quite dissimilar. The student therefore learns a geometry-preserving map of the teacher's representation space without ever needing to reconstruct individual activations exactly — a conceptually cleaner objective that also tends to be more robust to capacity mismatches between the two networks.

Tian, Krishnan, and Isola's Contrastive Representation Distillation extends the relational view using the contrastive self-supervised machinery that was later popularized by SimCLR and MoCo, whose derivations we worked through earlier in this series. The idea is to treat the student and teacher representations of the same image as a positive pair and the student's representation of one image against the teacher's representation of a different image as a negative pair. Maximizing a mutual information lower bound through an InfoNCE-style objective then encourages the student's representation to contain all the information present in the teacher's representation. The method performs well even when the teacher and student belong to entirely different architectural families — for instance, a ResNet teacher distilling into a MobileNet student — because it never requires hidden dimensions to align and the contrastive objective has a clean probabilistic grounding. There is also an illuminating connection to the self-supervised material we covered earlier: DINOv2's self-distillation mechanism, where the online network provides regression targets to itself via an exponential moving average of weights, is best understood as a special case of relational distillation in which the teacher and student share an architecture but differ in how their weights are temporally aggregated — the EMA teacher is a slowly moving anchor whose geometry the online student is trained to match.

One subtlety worth dwelling on is the role of the teacher's calibration quality. A teacher that is overconfident produces near-argmax soft outputs even at moderate temperature, which severely limits the dark-knowledge signal the student can extract. Work on combining label smoothing with distillation has shown that deliberately smoothing teacher logits before distillation improves student outcomes, effectively fusing two regularization strategies into a single target distribution. For vision transformers specifically, DeiT introduced a distillation token — a learnable vector appended to the patch embedding sequence — whose prediction is supervised by the teacher's hard argmax rather than soft probabilities, while the class token retains the standard label objective. This asymmetric treatment lets the model internalize complementary signals from both the label space and the teacher's decision boundary, often yielding better accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs than either signal alone. The distillation token also demonstrates that the ViT architecture's modularity — the fact that you can append arbitrary extra tokens to the sequence without architectural surgery — extends naturally beyond position encodings and [CLS] tokens to supervision channels, a flexibility that patch-based sequences afford in ways dense convolutional feature maps do not.
