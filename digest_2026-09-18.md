# Neural Radiance Fields Part II: Hierarchical Sampling, Mip-NeRF's Anti-Aliasing, and the Multiresolution Hash Encoding That Made Training Instant

**Date:** 2026-09-18
**Arc:** 2/2 — Neural Radiance Fields

---

The previous session derived the foundational NeRF framework: a multilayer perceptron maps a 5D input — three spatial coordinates and two viewing-direction angles — to an emitted radiance and volume density, and a differentiable volume-rendering integral accumulates color along each camera ray through classic alpha-compositing. The representation is compelling because it produces photorealistic novel views from a sparse set of training images without explicit geometry, but its practical limitations were severe: training a single scene required hundreds of thousands of gradient steps and a full day of GPU computation, and the vanilla sampling scheme wasted most evaluations on empty space or occluded regions that contribute little to the final render. The second generation of NeRF research addressed these inefficiencies through a combination of smarter sampling, principled treatment of scale, and a fundamental rethinking of the underlying input representation.

The original paper's partial solution to sampling efficiency was hierarchical sampling, sometimes called the coarse-to-fine strategy. A coarse network is first trained on uniformly sampled points along each ray, producing a rough estimate of where density accumulates. The estimated density is then treated as a probability distribution over ray depth, and additional fine sample points are drawn by importance sampling from this distribution — concentrating evaluation budget where the scene's actual geometry and appearance lie. A fine network trained on the union of coarse and importance-sampled points then produces the final rendered color. The coarse network acts as a learned proposal distribution, and the scheme reduces the fraction of evaluations wasted on empty space. It is, however, still sampling points rather than integrating over intervals, and this distinction becomes critical when images of a scene are captured at varying scales or resolutions.

The aliasing problem latent in point sampling became explicit when researchers tried to train NeRF on multiscale datasets — images captured at different distances or with different field-of-view settings. A single ray fired through a distant pixel subtends a large frustum of 3D space, but vanilla NeRF evaluates the radiance field at a single point on that ray, which is precisely what a pixel-level sample represents in a high-resolution image. When the same scene is represented at multiple scales, points that lie inside the same frustum at one scale are sampled separately at another, causing the MLP to produce inconsistent outputs and training to exhibit the characteristic zipper artifacts and blurring associated with aliasing. Barron and colleagues introduced Mip-NeRF to address this rigorously. Rather than casting rays made of infinitesimally thin point samples, they cast conical frustums — sections of cones that grow wider as they travel from the camera — and integrate the positional encoding over the frustum's volume rather than evaluating it at a point. The key insight is that the expected value of the positional encoding sinusoids over a Gaussian approximation to the cone section can be computed in closed form: each frequency component is multiplied by an attenuation factor proportional to exp(−2π²σ²f²), where σ² is the variance of the cone cross-section at that depth and f is the frequency. Higher frequencies are attenuated more at larger scales, exactly replicating the behavior of a proper prefiltered mipmap hierarchy. The integrated positional encoding thus tells the network not only where in space to evaluate, but at what effective scale, allowing a single network to represent the scene consistently across all viewing distances and resolutions simultaneously.

Even with these architectural refinements, NeRF's central bottleneck remained the MLP itself: every query to the radiance field requires a forward pass through a deep network, and rendering a single image requires millions of such queries. The runtime of training and inference scales with network depth, width, and the number of ray samples per pixel, with no obvious way to break this coupling. Müller and colleagues' Instant-NGP, published at SIGGRAPH 2022, attacked this bottleneck by replacing the large MLP with a much smaller one — often just one or two layers — augmented by a learned multiresolution feature grid that provides highly compressed spatial lookup tables. The feature grid operates at L levels of geometric resolution, each defined by a grid of trainable feature vectors in a hash table of fixed size T. Given a 3D query coordinate, the system finds the eight surrounding grid vertices at each resolution level, trilinearly interpolates the feature vectors stored at those vertices, and concatenates the interpolated features across all levels into a single input vector for the MLP. The hash collision problem — two spatially distant grid vertices at a coarse level sharing the same hash bucket — turns out to be benign in practice, because the subsequent fine-level features disambiguate, and the MLP learns to integrate contributions from all levels coherently.

The dramatic speedup from Instant-NGP comes from two sources. First, the feature lookup is extraordinarily cache-friendly: after one forward pass, the relevant hash entries for a given scene region are in GPU L2 cache, so subsequent passes over nearby pixels incur minimal memory latency. Second, the tiny MLP requires far fewer FLOPS per query than the original NeRF architecture, meaning that throughput per second is orders of magnitude higher. Together, these advantages reduce training from hours to tens of seconds while maintaining rendering quality comparable to the original system — a 1000-fold speedup that moved NeRF from a research curiosity to a usable reconstruction pipeline. This is worth examining pedagogically, because it illustrates a common pattern in the history of deep learning: a conceptually elegant but computationally infeasible idea (implicit neural representation) is made practical not by changing the objective but by changing the representation — trading a uniform, parameter-free signal (pure MLP) for a structured, cache-efficient one (hash-encoded feature grid plus a small MLP).

A third line of improvement addresses the gap between laboratory NeRF setups — controlled lighting, static scenes, known camera poses — and real-world photo collections. NeRF in the Wild by Martin-Brualla and colleagues handles the fact that internet photo collections of landmarks are captured under wildly varying lighting conditions, at different times of day, and with transient occluders like tourists who appear in some images but not others. Their key contribution is separating each scene into a static component that contributes to the reconstructed radiance field, and a per-image appearance embedding that modulates the static component through a learned affine transformation of intermediate features. The appearance embedding absorbs variation in global illumination, color balance, and weather, while a separate transient head predicts which scene elements are likely to be occlusions in any given image and down-weights their contribution to the reconstruction loss. This decomposition is additive and clean: the model is otherwise identical to standard NeRF, so persistent scene geometry and per-image photometric variability are separated without requiring explicit illumination modeling or physics-based rendering.

Looking at this body of work together reveals a progression that carries directly into the explicit representation paradigm. Each generation identified the binding constraint of the previous one: vanilla NeRF spent evaluations on empty space, solved by hierarchical sampling; it aliased across scales, solved by integrated positional encoding; it was slow because of large MLPs, solved by hash-encoded feature grids; it failed on unconstrained collections, addressed by appearance embeddings. What none of these approaches escaped was the rendering bottleneck of volume integration — every view synthesis requires summing contributions from hundreds of samples per pixel, which, even with Instant-NGP's speed, limits interactive rendering to specialized hardware. Explicit representations that allow direct rasterization rather than volumetric integration address this final bottleneck, and that is precisely the motivation behind 3D Gaussian Splatting, which discards the implicit MLP entirely in favor of a set of anisotropic Gaussians rendered through differentiable splatting — the approach we examined immediately after this arc in the broader curriculum.
